# Speed-Feed Applied Practice

The practitioner-knowledge layer of the SFC (Speed-Feed Calculator) galaxy: the tribal knowledge a world-class speed/feed expert carries that pure theory does not teach -- common **failure modes**, **gotchas**, and **technique decisions**. Theory (Kienzle / Taylor / chip-thinning formula structure) lives in [`speed-feed-foundations.md`](speed-feed-foundations.md); the living link directory lives in [`speed-feed-source-atlas.md`](speed-feed-source-atlas.md). This entry is **what goes wrong and how an expert avoids it** -- each note states the gotcha, *why* it happens, and the avoidance, all qualitatively. No cutting number is promoted; every numeric value stays owner-gated in `mcp-server/src/physics/constants.ts`.

## Common failure modes

- **Tool deflection scales with stickout/overhang -- and it lies to you as taper, not always as breakage.** The more tool hangs out of the holder (the less shank gripped), the more the tool bends under cutting load. The dangerous part is the *quiet* failure: deflection bends a wall into a **taper** and pushes the cut off its target dimension long before the tool snaps, so a part can be scrapped for size while the tool still looks fine. **Avoidance (direction of trade-off):** chuck the tool as deep as possible (minimize overhang), use the **largest diameter** the feature allows, prefer **necked/reached** tooling over **long-flute** tooling when depth is needed (long flutes thin the core, which raises deflection), and reach with the **smallest reach + length-of-cut on the largest diameter**. Higher stickout -> more deflection -> step back to lighter radial/axial engagement, not more feed. The vendor's deflection magnitude is owner-gated; the relationship is monotonic and promoted here. ([Harvey In The Loupe -- Tool Deflection & Its Remedies](https://www.harveyperformance.com/in-the-loupe/tool-deflection-remedies/))

- **Chip recutting / chip packing destroys finish and tools in enclosed cuts.** When chips are not evacuated, the cutter re-cuts its own swarf -- this packs the flutes, spikes heat, ruins finish, and breaks edges, and it is worst in slots and pockets where the chip has nowhere to go. **Why:** aggressive speeds/feeds beyond the tool's capability plus too-small flute gullets or weak coolant let chips re-enter the cut. **Avoidance:** switch to an end mill with **fewer flutes** (bigger gullets evacuate better), raise **coolant volume/pressure** and move the **nozzle closer** to the cut, and prefer **climb (down) milling** so chips drop *behind* the cutter instead of being carried back into the cut. ([Harvey In The Loupe -- End Mill Troubleshooting Guide](https://www.harveyperformance.com/in-the-loupe/end-mill-troubleshooting-guide/))

- **Premature tool wear comes from running too LIGHT, not only too hard.** The intuitive failure is "ran it too fast and burned the edge," but an expert knows the *other* tail is just as deadly: running speed/feed **too light** lets the tool rub instead of cut, generating heat and recutting chips that wear the edge fast. So wear is a two-sided gotcha -- both excessive and insufficient speed/feed accelerate it. **Avoidance:** keep the cutter genuinely *cutting* (positive, real chip load), pick the right coating/helix for the material, and adjust depth of cut so chips clear. The "recommended" speed/feed band that sits between the two failure tails is owner-gated. ([Harvey In The Loupe -- End Mill Troubleshooting Guide](https://www.harveyperformance.com/in-the-loupe/end-mill-troubleshooting-guide/))

- **Chatter is a rigidity problem masquerading as a speeds-and-feeds problem.** Self-excited vibration leaves a wavy, poor finish and chips edges. **Why:** insufficient rigidity in tool/holder/workpiece/machine, an over-aggressive toolpath, or wrong tool geometry -- *not primarily* a "wrong RPM" issue, though speed interacts with it. **Avoidance:** reduce speed/feed/depth-of-cut, swap to a **shorter** tool or a more rigid holder, check **spindle run-out**, and **re-fixture** the part for stability before reaching for a different feed number. ([Harvey In The Loupe -- End Mill Troubleshooting Guide](https://www.harveyperformance.com/in-the-loupe/end-mill-troubleshooting-guide/))

## Materials gotchas

- **Built-up edge (BUE) is a LOW-speed / gummy-material failure -- speed *up* to escape it.** On ductile, "gummy" materials (low-carbon steel, austenitic stainless, aluminum) at low cutting speed, chips cold-weld to the cutting edge; when that welded lump tears off it rips a fleck of carbide with it, pitting the edge and wrecking surface finish and size. **Why:** the cutting-zone temperature is *too low* for the material to shear cleanly, so it adheres. **Avoidance (counter-intuitive direction):** *raise* cutting speed to lift the zone temperature out of the BUE-prone window, run a **sharp positive-rake / polished or post-treated** edge, and use lubrication to fight adhesion. This is the one place where "go faster" is the fix, not the cause -- the inverse of the burn-the-edge intuition. The specific speed above which BUE subsides for a given material is owner-gated. ([Machining Doctor -- Built-Up Edge (BUE)](https://www.machiningdoctor.com/glossary/built-up-edge-bue/))

- **Work-hardening alloys punish dwelling and too-light feed -- you must cut UNDER the skin.** Austenitic stainless (304/316), Inconel/Monel, titanium, and other high-ductility alloys harden the surface as you deform it; if the tool rubs, dwells, or runs too light it work-hardens the skin without removing it, leaving a hardened layer the next pass must fight through -- driving up force, heat, and flank wear. **Why:** plastic deformation without chip removal strain-hardens the subsurface. **Avoidance:** keep a **positive, sufficient chip load** so each edge cuts *below* the previously-hardened layer, **never dwell** (keep the tool moving), use **sharp** tools (less force = less rubbing), and avoid any condition where the tool burnishes instead of cuts. The qualitative rule: in these alloys, too-light is more dangerous than slightly-heavy. ([Machining Doctor -- Work Hardening](https://www.machiningdoctor.com/glossary/work-hardening/))

- **Recutting + adhesion compound on sticky materials.** On adhesion-prone materials (aluminum especially), letting the cutter recut the same chips repeatedly is a fast path to built-up edge -- the two failure modes reinforce each other. **Why:** re-contacting hot, already-deformed chips raises the chance of cold-welding to the edge. **Avoidance:** treat chip evacuation as the first priority (clear the chip before it can re-weld) and use lubrication to break the material's affinity for the tool. ([CNCCookbook -- CNC Coolant Best Practices](https://www.cnccookbook.com/cnc-coolant-system-mist-high-pressure-through-spindle/))

## Technique decisions

- **Chip thinning: a light radial cut quietly STARVES the chip, so you must feed *more*, not less.** When the radial depth (stepover) drops below the cutter radius, the actual chip comes out *thinner* than the programmed feed-per-tooth -- so a feed number that looks safe is really making the tool **rub**, generating heat and wearing the edge. The trap is that the operator's instinct on a "light" cut is to back the feed off, which makes it worse. **Avoidance (direction):** *increase* feed-per-tooth to compensate for the geometric thinning and restore a real chip; this is the engine of High Efficiency Milling -- pair a **light radial** with a **deep axial** so wear spreads across the full length of cut instead of concentrating in a band, extending tool life at high MRR. The chip-thinning *factor* and the corrected feed are computed from the formula in [`speed-feed-foundations.md`](speed-feed-foundations.md); the resulting numbers are owner-gated. ([Harvey In The Loupe -- High Efficiency Milling](https://www.harveyperformance.com/in-the-loupe/high-efficiency-milling/))

- **Flute count is a chip-space-vs-strength decision keyed to material -- not a finish preference.** Fewer flutes (e.g. 2-3) suit aluminum/non-ferrous because the **deeper flute valleys** evacuate the large chips that soft material throws at high feed; more flutes (4+) suit steel/ferrous because the harder material needs a **larger, stronger core** (which shrinks the valleys -- acceptable because ferrous chips are smaller) and the extra edges add finish and productivity. **The trade-off:** a bigger core = stronger tool but *less* chip room; choosing too many flutes for aluminum packs chips, choosing too few for hardened steel wastes core strength. **Avoidance:** pick flute count from the material's chip-evacuation demand first, tool strength second, finish third. ([Harvey In The Loupe -- Why Flute Count Matters](https://www.harveyperformance.com/in-the-loupe/flute-count-matters/))

- **Coolant delivery is a deliberate choice, and with carbide the worst choice is a WEAK one.** The delivery method is a real decision -- **air blast** (chip clearing + cool, no lube, good where thermal shock is a risk), **mist** (light cooling/lube, light cuts), **flood** (lube + chip flush, the general default), **high-pressure / through-tool** (blasts chips out of deep holes and pockets). **The carbide gotcha:** carbide is brittle and cracks under **thermal shock**, so *inconsistent* coolant -- random droplets hitting a hot edge -- micro-cracks the carbide and can be **worse than running dry**. **Avoidance:** with carbide, commit -- either **copious consistent flood** or **dry/air**, never a weak intermittent stream; treat **chip evacuation as the higher priority than cooling** for carbide (most heat leaves with the chip); and for **poor-heat-conductors like titanium**, flood is essentially mandatory because the part cannot shed heat on its own. The exact pressures/flows are owner-gated. ([Harvey In The Loupe -- Coolant for CNC Machining](https://www.harveyperformance.com/in-the-loupe/coolant-for-cnc-machining/)) ([CNCCookbook -- CNC Coolant Best Practices](https://www.cnccookbook.com/cnc-coolant-system-mist-high-pressure-through-spindle/))

- **Climb (down) milling is the default for finish, heat, and chip handling -- with one fixturing caveat.** Climb milling enters at maximum chip thickness and exits at zero (thick-to-thin), which transfers heat **into the chip** rather than the work/tool, gives a cleaner shear plane, reduces rubbing and recutting, and extends tool life. **The caveat (technique decision):** climb-milling forces tend to **pull the workpiece into the cutter / press it down**, so the fixture must resist pull-in (and on a worn machine with backlash, climb can chatter). Up (conventional) milling is the deliberate exception -- preferred on hard scaled/cast surfaces, large stock-allowance variation, and impact-sensitive ceramic inserts in heat-resistant alloys. **Avoidance:** default to climb for finish and tool life; reach for conventional only for those specific exceptions. (Force-direction + thick-to-thin mechanics cross-referenced from the Sandvik framing in [`speed-feed-foundations.md`](speed-feed-foundations.md); the recutting-reduction benefit is confirmed here.) ([Harvey In The Loupe -- End Mill Troubleshooting Guide](https://www.harveyperformance.com/in-the-loupe/end-mill-troubleshooting-guide/))

## Setup, fixturing & verification

- **Spindle/holder run-out silently overloads one flute and halves your tool life -- check it before blaming the feed.** Run-out (the tool wobbling off its true axis) makes some edges cut at a larger radius than others, so a "balanced" chip load is in reality dumped onto **one flute**. That overloaded edge wears or breaks early, finish suffers, and effective tool life drops -- and it hits **small-diameter tools hardest** because the same wobble is a larger fraction of the cut. **Why:** debris in the collet, a worn/cheap holder, or a damaged interface throws the tool off concentric. **Avoidance:** keep **collets clean**, use **quality precision-ground holders**, and **verify with a dial indicator** before a production run -- a run-out problem will never be fixed by re-tuning speeds and feeds. ([Machining Doctor -- Runout](https://www.machiningdoctor.com/glossary/runout/))

- **Poor finish: lower the feed and raise the speed -- and suspect a dull edge first.** A rough finish usually means feed is too aggressive and/or speed too slow for the pass, *or* the edge is simply worn/dull. **Avoidance:** lower feed and depth-of-cut, raise spindle speed, and -- critically -- use a **sharp or freshly reground** tool, because no speed/feed change rescues a dull edge. The specific finishing speed/feed band is owner-gated. ([Harvey In The Loupe -- End Mill Troubleshooting Guide](https://www.harveyperformance.com/in-the-loupe/end-mill-troubleshooting-guide/))
