## Surface speed / feed kinematics (RPM <-> SFM)

- **Spindle speed from surface speed.** Surface speed, spindle RPM, and tool diameter are related by the constant 3.82 (= 12/pi for inch units): cutter diameter x RPM / 3.82 = SFM, which rearranges to **RPM = SFM x 3.82 / D** (D in inches). Equivalently `RPM = (SFM x 12) / (pi x D)`. Smaller tools must run at higher RPM for the same surface speed.
- **Feed-rate chain.** Table feed builds from chip load per tooth multiplied by the number of flutes (then by RPM) to reach inches-per-minute. The vendor caution applies: a feed published *per revolution* must NOT be re-multiplied by flute count.
- _Confirmed (partial): Harvey Performance "Speeds and Feeds 101" confirms the `cutter diameter x RPM / 3.82 = SFM` relationship and the chip-load-per-tooth x number-of-flutes feed structure in body text. The numeric chip-load band (e.g. 0.001-0.010 in/tooth) is NOT promoted — see Owner-gate._ ([Harvey Performance — Speeds and Feeds 101](https://www.harveyperformance.com/in-the-loupe/speeds-and-feeds-101/))

## Kienzle specific-cutting-force LAW (structure only)

- **Power-law form.** The specific cutting force `KC` is not constant; it follows a power law in the uncut chip thickness `h` (the curve slope, not the chip width, drives it): **KC = KC1.1 x h^(-MC)**, where `MC` is the "curve slope of the KC graph" describing how KC varies as chip thickness moves away from the 1 mm reference point.
- **Definition of KC1.1.** `KC1.1` is the normalized specific cutting force — the force in the cutting direction to remove a chip area of one square millimetre at 1 mm thickness, at a 0 degree top rake angle.
- **Vendor rake-angle correction.** Catalog practice extends the bare law with a top-rake correction term: **KC = KC1.1 x h^(-MC) x (1 - 0.01 x GAMF)**, where `GAMF` is the top rake angle in degrees.
- _Confirmed (structure only): Machining Doctor "Specific Cutting Force (KC & KC1)" confirms the `KC = KC1.1 x h^(-MC) x (1 - 0.01 x GAMF)` form, the KC1.1 reference-geometry definition, and the MC = curve-slope meaning. The numeric KC1.1 / MC material values are NOT promoted — PRISM sources those from `constants.ts` only._ ([Machining Doctor — Specific Cutting Force (KC & KC1)](https://www.machiningdoctor.com/glossary/specific-cutting-force-kc-kc1/))

## Taylor tool-life EQUATION (structure only)

- **Basic Taylor equation.** **V x T^n = C**, where V = cutting speed (m/min), T = tool life (min), `n` = a constant ("tool life index") whose value depends on the cutting-tool and job material, and `C` = the machining constant. Higher cutting speed shortens tool life.
- **Extended / generalized form.** To include feed and depth of cut: **V x T^n x f^n1 x d^n2 = C**, where `f` = feed (mm/rev) and `d` = depth of cut (mm), each with its own exponent.
- _Confirmed (structure only): Mechical "Taylor's Tool Life Equation" confirms both `VT^n = C` (with the V/T/n/C definitions) and the extended `VT^n x f^n1 x d^n2 = C` form. The numeric `n` exponent ranges per tool material (Fact 6) and the speed > feed > depth ordering (Fact 7) were NOT confirmed on this page and stay owner-gated._ ([Mechical — Taylor's Tool Life Equation](https://www.mechical.com/2022/01/tool-life-taylors-tool-life-equation.html))

## Radial chip-thinning FACTOR (structure + method)

- **When it applies.** When the radial depth of cut `Ae` is smaller than the cutter radius (i.e. below 50% of diameter `D`), the actual maximum chip thickness becomes smaller than the programmed feed per tooth — the chip-thinning effect — so feed must be increased to keep true chip thickness on target.
- **The factor.** **RCTF = 1 / sqrt(1 - [1 - (2 x Ae/D)]^2)**. It equals 1 once `Ae >= R` (cutter radius), where the maximum chip thickness is constant and equal to the feed per tooth.
- **Combining with axial.** For round / ballnose tools, the radial and axial factors are independent and applied on top of each other: **CTF = RCTF x ACTF**, and effective feed **Fz = recommended chip load x CTF**.
- _Confirmed (structure + method): Machining Doctor "Chip Thinning Calculators and Formulas" confirms the RCTF formula, the `Ae >= R` unity threshold, the independent `CTF = RCTF x ACTF` composition, and `Fz = chip load x CTF`. Quick numeric rules-of-thumb (e.g. "25% stepover -> +30% feed") are NOT promoted — see Owner-gate._ ([Machining Doctor — Chip Thinning Calculators and Formulas](https://www.machiningdoctor.com/calculators/chip-thinning-calculator/))

## Orthogonal cutting + Merchant shear-angle MODEL (theory, structure only)

- **Orthogonal cutting geometry.** Metal cutting is modeled as a tool with a defined **rake angle (alpha)** removing a chip; the chip forms by shear in a narrow zone near the tool tip oriented at the **shear angle (phi)**. The shear becomes large for small or negative rake angles.
- **Chip thickness ratio + shear strain.** The deformed (post-cut) chip thickness relates to the original undeformed depth of cut by the **chip thickness ratio**; the shear strain in the primary deformation zone is derived geometrically from that ratio together with the rake and shear angles.
- **Force decomposition.** The resultant cutting force decomposes into a **cutting force** (along the cutting direction), a **thrust force** (perpendicular to it), and a **shear force** acting along the shear plane. Shear stress on the shear plane = **shear force / shear area**, where the shear area is the undeformed-chip cross-section divided by sin(phi) over the full width of cut.
- **Merchant minimum-energy criterion.** The **Merchant model** predicts the shear angle by the principle that phi adjusts to **minimize the total energy dissipated** in cutting, giving a relationship in which the shear angle varies with the rake angle and the friction angle (beta) — increasing rake angle raises the shear angle; increasing friction lowers it. This is the same `phi = f(alpha, beta)` structure left gated as Fact 8 above; the MIT lecture confirms the model's STRUCTURE and minimum-energy derivation qualitatively (the explicit `45 + alpha/2 - beta/2` closed form is still owner-gated, not promoted as a number here).
- **Specific cutting energy.** Cutting energy per unit volume of material removed is computed from the force relationships and tool geometry — the qualitative bridge to the Kienzle specific-force law above (specific energy rises as uncut chip thickness falls — the size-effect direction, qualitatively).
- _Confirmed (theory + structure): MIT 2.810 "Subtractive Processes: Machining" (T. Gutowski) confirms orthogonal cutting geometry, the Merchant model and its minimum-energy criterion, the shear-angle dependence on rake + friction, shear strain from the chip-thickness ratio, the cutting/thrust/shear force decomposition, shear-stress = shear-force/shear-area, and the specific-cutting-energy concept. No numeric material constant promoted._ ([MIT 2.810 — Subtractive Processes: Machining (lecture PDF)](https://web.mit.edu/2.810/www/files/lectures/lec5-machining-2018.pdf))

## Process-attribute framing (how to evaluate any machining process)

- **Four attributes.** A manufacturing process is evaluated on **quality, rate (throughput), cost, and flexibility** — the trade-off space every speed/feed decision implicitly navigates (a faster feed raises rate but can cost quality and tool life).
- **Physics + variation.** Process behavior is governed by physics but is subject to **inherent variation/randomness**, so deterministic physics must be paired with statistical thinking — this is the qualitative justification for treating speed/feed recommendations as centers of a distribution, not exact values, and for the existence of `material_calibrated` / confidence flags in the SFC engines.
- **Material removal rate.** **MRR** is the characteristic throughput metric for subtractive processes — the quantity a speed/feed optimizer is maximizing subject to the safety constraints.
- _Confirmed (framing): MIT 2.810 intro lecture confirms quality/rate/cost/flexibility as the process-evaluation attributes, the physics-plus-randomness framing, and material removal rate as the subtractive-process throughput metric._ ([MIT 2.810 — Intro lecture (PDF)](https://web.mit.edu/2.810/www/files/lectures/lec1-intro-2019.pdf))

## Down (climb) vs up (conventional) milling — engagement METHOD

- **Definition (feed vs rotation).** In **down (climb) milling** the cutter is fed **in the direction of its rotation**; in **up (conventional) milling** the feed is **opposite** to rotation. Classification is by the feed direction of the workpiece relative to cutter rotation.
- **Chip-thickness direction ("thick to thin").** Down milling enters at **maximum chip thickness and exits at zero** (thick-to-thin — the "golden rule"), which avoids the edge rubbing/burnishing that wastes tool life. Up milling does the reverse: enters at **zero and increases to maximum at exit** (thin-to-thick), causing initial rubbing and higher tensile stress + temperature at exit.
- **Force direction + fixturing.** Down-milling cutting forces tend to **pull the workpiece into the cutter / press it down** onto the table (fixtures must resist pull-in); up-milling radial forces tend to **lift the workpiece off the table** (fixtures must resist lifting). The down-milling downforce can stabilize thin parts.
- **When to choose which.** Down milling is preferred whenever machine/fixture/workpiece allow (modern machines compensate backlash). Up milling is preferred for **large variation in working allowance**, for hard scaled/cast surfaces, and when running **ceramic inserts in heat-resistant alloys** (ceramics are impact-sensitive at workpiece entry).
- _Confirmed (method): Sandvik Coromant "Down milling vs. up milling" confirms the feed-vs-rotation definition, the thick-to-thin / thin-to-thick chip-thickness behavior, the pull-in vs lift force directions and fixture demands, and the application recommendations. No numeric constant promoted._ ([Sandvik Coromant — Down milling vs. up milling](https://www.sandvik.coromant.com/en-us/knowledge/milling/down-milling-vs-up-milling))

## Turning kinematics (formula STRUCTURE, turning analogue of the SFM/RPM chain)

- **Cutting speed <-> spindle speed.** For turning, surface cutting speed **Vc = pi x D x n** (D = workpiece diameter, n = spindle speed), with a unit-conversion divisor (1000 metric / 12 imperial) — the lathe analogue of the milling RPM<->SFM relationship already in this entry, but keyed on **workpiece** diameter rather than tool diameter.
- **Feed chain.** Linear feed velocity **Vf = n x Fn** (Fn = feed per revolution). Note the contrast with milling: turning feed is naturally per-revolution, so it is NOT multiplied by a flute count.
- **Material removal rate.** **Q = Vc x Fn x ap** (ap = depth of cut) — throughput as the product of speed, feed-per-rev, and depth.
- **Machining time + surface-finish structure.** Machining time = cut length / linear feed rate (resolving to `T = (l x pi x D)/(const x Fn x Vc)`); theoretical surface roughness follows **Ra is proportional to Fn^2 / r** (r = tool-nose radius) — finish degrades with the square of feed and improves with larger nose radius. (Structure only; the proportionality constant and any material Vc/Fn values stay owner-gated.)
- _Confirmed (structure only): Machining Doctor "Turning Calculators" confirms Vc = pi x D x n, Vf = n x Fn, Q = Vc x Fn x ap, the machining-time structure, and the Ra ~ Fn^2/r finish relationship. No numeric cutting constant promoted._ ([Machining Doctor — Turning Calculators](https://www.machiningdoctor.com/calculators/turning-calculators/))

## Residual stress: machining as a source + how it is MEASURED (gov standards framing)

- **Machining induces residual stress.** Cutting introduces residual stress through **mechanical deformation during cutting, thermal effects from friction/heat, and surface/near-surface material alteration** — i.e., the speed/feed/depth chosen leaves a stress signature in the finished surface, not just a dimension.
- **Why it matters.** Residual stress state drives **fatigue life** (compressive surface stress can enhance fatigue life; tensile reduces it) and overall **structural integrity / failure risk** when combined with service loads — the qualitative reason aggressive finishing parameters can be acceptable dimensionally yet harmful to part life.
- **Measurement methods (named).** The NASA handbook enumerates residual-stress measurement techniques: **hole drilling** (incremental, with strain gauges), **slitting**, the **contour method**, **X-ray diffraction** (near-surface lattice strain), and **neutron diffraction** (deeper subsurface mapping than X-ray). This gives the SFC galaxy a standards-grade vocabulary for any future surface-integrity verification surface.
- _Confirmed (gov report, method + framing): NASA-HDBK-5026 confirms machining as a residual-stress source (mechanical + thermal + surface alteration), the fatigue/structural-integrity significance, and the named measurement methods (hole drilling, slitting, contour, XRD, neutron diffraction). No numeric material constant promoted._ ([NASA-HDBK-5026 — Structural integrity / residual stress handbook (PDF)](https://standards.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/standards/NASA/Baseline/0/NASA-HDBK-5026-Final-8-12-2024.pdf))

## Spindle torque + power kinematics (rotational dynamics STRUCTURE)

- **Rotational Newton's second law.** A spindle/cutter is a rigid body in rotation, governed by **tau_net = I x alpha** (net torque = moment of inertia x angular acceleration) — the rotational analogue of `F = m x a`, obtained by multiplying the translational law by the lever arm `r` (force -> torque, mass -> moment of inertia, linear accel -> angular accel). Moment of inertia is the "resistance to change of rotational motion."
- **Torque from force at a radius.** When force is perpendicular to the radius, **tau = r x F** — the torque about the spindle axis equals cutting force times the radial lever arm. This is the bridge from a tangential cutting force (the Kienzle `KC` x chip-area product elsewhere in this entry) to the torque the spindle must supply.
- **Spindle power = torque x angular velocity.** Rotational power follows **P = tau x omega** (instantaneous power = torque x angular speed), with rotational work **W = integral of tau dtheta**. This is the formula STRUCTURE behind the SFC's required-spindle-power check: at a fixed cutting torque, power demand scales directly with spindle speed — so pushing RPM for surface speed raises the kW/HP the machine must deliver, a hard machine-side constraint on any speed recommendation.
- _Confirmed (structure only): OpenStax University Physics Vol. 1 confirms tau_net = I x alpha (and its derivation from F = ma via the lever arm), tau = rF for perpendicular force, the rotational power relation P = tau x omega, and rotational work W = integral tau dtheta. No numeric constant promoted._ ([OpenStax University Physics Vol. 1 — 10.7 Newton's Second Law for Rotation](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/10-7-newtons-second-law-for-rotation)) ([OpenStax University Physics Vol. 1 — 10.8 Work and Power for Rotational Motion](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/10-8-work-and-power-for-rotational-motion))

## Cutting-zone friction + heat (the physics behind tool-chip contact and thermal limits)

- **Friction law structure (tool-chip + flank).** Friction force is proportional to the normal force: **kinetic friction f_k = mu_k x N**, while static friction is a responsive ceiling **f_s <= mu_s x N** (mu_s > mu_k). The coefficient mu is dimensionless and material-pair-dependent. Crucially, the OpenStax microscopic model — surfaces touch only at high-spot asperities, and real contact area grows with normal force — is why friction depends on the normal load (not nominal area). This is the qualitative basis for the **friction angle (beta)** that enters the Merchant shear-angle model already in this entry: the tool-chip interface is a friction pair, and beta is its `arctan(mu)` analogue.
- **Heat generation from cutting work.** Heat transferred relates to a temperature rise by **Q = m x c x deltaT** (heat = mass x specific heat x temperature change). In machining, the plastic-deformation + friction work converts to heat in the shear zone and at the tool-chip interface; a low-specific-heat or low-mass chip carries away less heat per degree, raising interface temperature. This is the STRUCTURE behind why aggressive MRR (more material work per unit time) drives the cutting-zone temperature that ultimately limits speed via thermal tool wear.
- **Thermal growth / dimensional drift.** Unconstrained material expands by **deltaL = alpha x L x deltaT** (linear thermal expansion: change in length = coefficient x original length x temperature change), proportional in all dimensions. The cutting-zone heat above therefore feeds back into part + spindle + tool dimensional growth — the qualitative justification for thermal-soak and warm-up considerations in any high-accuracy speed/feed regime.
- _Confirmed (structure only): OpenStax University Physics confirms f_k = mu_k N / f_s <= mu_s N with the asperity contact-area model (Vol. 1, 6.2), Q = m c deltaT heat-transfer structure (Vol. 2, 1.4), and deltaL = alpha L deltaT linear thermal expansion (Vol. 2, 1.3). No numeric coefficient promoted._ ([OpenStax University Physics Vol. 1 — 6.2 Friction](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/6-2-friction)) ([OpenStax University Physics Vol. 2 — 1.4 Heat Transfer, Specific Heat, and Calorimetry](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/1-4-heat-transfer-specific-heat-and-calorimetry)) ([OpenStax University Physics Vol. 2 — 1.3 Thermal Expansion](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/1-3-thermal-expansion))

## Statistical basis for "recommendation = a distribution, not a number"

- **Standardizing a value (z-score).** A measured value's position relative to a process is captured by **z = (x - mu) / sigma** — how many standard deviations the value sits above/below the mean — with the inverse **x = mu + z x sigma**. The z-transform lets data scaled differently be compared on a common reference frame (the standard normal).
- **Why it matters for SFC.** This is the formula STRUCTURE that justifies the SFC engines treating a recommended speed/feed as the **center of a distribution** with confidence bands rather than a single exact value (the `material_calibrated` / confidence flags noted in the MIT 2.810 process-attribute section above). A parameter that is, say, 2-sigma into the aggressive tail of the historically-survivable regime is a quantitatively different risk than one at the mean — the z-score is the tool that makes "how far from typical" a number, not a feeling. (No process mu/sigma values are promoted; only the standardization STRUCTURE.)
- _Confirmed (structure only): OpenStax Introductory Statistics confirms the z-score z = (x - mu)/sigma, its inverse x = mu + z*sigma, the "standard deviations from the mean" interpretation, and the common-reference-frame purpose. No numeric value promoted._ ([OpenStax Introductory Statistics — 6.1 The Standard Normal Distribution](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics/pages/6-1-the-standard-normal-distribution))
