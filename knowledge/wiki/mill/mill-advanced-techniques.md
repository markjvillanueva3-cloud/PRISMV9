## Theme A -- Engagement-managed roughing (the modern high-MRR family)

The unifying idea of this family: the limiting factor in roughing is not how much material you take per pass, it is how much of the cutting EDGE is engaged and how concentrated the heat/force is on it. The state-of-the-art strategies all DEFEAT the old "wider radial cut = more metal" instinct by controlling the radial engagement deliberately.

### High Efficiency Milling (HEM) -- invert the depth-of-cut ratio
- **The technique:** HEM is a roughing strategy that uses a LOWER radial depth of cut (RDOC) and a HIGHER axial depth of cut (ADOC) -- the reverse of traditional milling's shallow-and-wide approach. By taking many light radial passes down the FULL flute length, it "spreads wear evenly across the cutting edge, dissipates heat, and reduces the chance of tool failure," whereas conventional milling concentrates heat "in one small portion of the cutting tool, expediting the tool wear process." (Confirmed verbatim.)
- **WHEN the expert uses it:** bulk roughing / pocketing where tool life and throughput both matter, and especially in heat-sensitive or difficult materials. HEM is the umbrella the CAM vendors ship under proprietary names ("Dynamic Milling", "Adaptive", "VoluMill", etc.).
- **Why it works -- the foundation it stands on:** it is "largely based on the theory surrounding Radial Chip Thinning" (the MECHANISM lives in `mill-foundations.md`): because the radial cut is below the cutter radius, the actual chip is thinner than the programmed feed per tooth, so the feed must be raised to keep a constant maximum chip thickness through the whole operation.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** accept MORE axial passes (more depth-wise motion) to take LESS radial bite -> distributed heat/wear, longer tool life, higher sustained MRR. The cost is that it depends on rigid clamping and a controller/tool that can hold the high feeds. (The RDOC/ADOC percentages and chip-load values are owner-gated.)
- Source: [Harvey Performance / In The Loupe -- Introduction to High Efficiency Milling](https://www.harveyperformance.com/in-the-loupe/intro-high-efficiency-milling/).

### Trochoidal milling -- circular-path slotting instead of full-width plunge
- **The technique:** rather than driving the cutter straight down a slot at full width, the tool follows continuous LOOPING / circular arcs, advancing a little each loop -- keeping a LOW radial depth of cut and a HIGH axial depth of cut, in continuous high-speed motion. This lets you "machine a slot wider than the cutting tool's cutting diameter." (Confirmed.)
- **WHEN the expert uses it:** deep slots, grooves, and confined cavities; slots WIDER than the cutter; and notoriously, hard / exotic materials (Inconel, titanium, hardened steel) where a conventional full-slot cut would over-heat and weld chips. It is the slotting member of the HEM family.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** the minimal radial engagement "decreases the amount of heat produced in the cut while also decreasing the cutting forces and load on the spindle," distributes wear along the full edge, and the spiral keeps clearing chips -- "dramatically improved tool life" and faster cycle time. The cost is a much longer total path with a lot more rapid reversals, so it "sacrifices single-pass efficiency for superior tool longevity and reduced machine stress" (and puts more wear-and-tear on ballscrews/slideways at the high feeds involved). (Cutter-to-slot ratio and stepover numbers are owner-gated.)
- **This PRISM galaxy applies it:** the mill galaxy's slotting / pocket-roughing toolpath logic should reach for trochoidal whenever the requested slot is wider than the chosen cutter or the material is hard/exotic, rather than emitting a full-immersion slot.
- Sources: [Harvey Performance / In The Loupe -- Intro to Trochoidal Milling](https://www.harveyperformance.com/in-the-loupe/introduction-trochoidal-milling/) ; [Gosiger -- Why You Should Consider Trochoidal Milling](https://www.gosiger.com/news/bid/152475/why-you-should-consider-trochoidal-milling) (independent second confirmation -- machine-tool distributor).

### Adaptive clearing / constant-tool-engagement HSM toolpaths
- **The technique:** a high-speed-machining roughing path whose explicit goal is to "maintain a constant chip load" / constant tool engagement angle throughout the cut, "while minimizing feed rate losses." The path uses smooth, curved, arc-based transitions instead of sharp directional changes, and never lets the tool slam from open air into full-immersion engagement. (Confirmed.)
- **WHEN the expert uses it:** any roughing where corners and pockets would otherwise SPIKE the engagement -- a full-width slotting entry "force[s] the tool into immediate, maximum material engagement, spiking the cutting forces and thermal stress beyond sustainable levels," and a sharp internal corner makes the look-ahead controller "automatically reduce the feed rate" defensively. Adaptive paths arc/loop into those corners so the engagement never spikes.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** "smoother paths = more NC code but fluid cutting action" and "avoiding full-immersion entry = more passes required but lower per-pass force concentration." Because the engagement (and therefore force) is constant, the controller does not need to throttle the feed, so the net result is "reduced cycle time and tool cost despite potentially more total passes."
- **This PRISM galaxy applies it:** when the mill galaxy plans a pocket/corner, the strategy layer should prefer an engagement-controlled path over a raster that would over-engage at corners -- the constant-engagement assumption is also what makes the HEM/trochoidal feed-up math valid.
- Source: [Modern Machine Shop -- Tool Path Strategies for High Speed Machining](https://www.mmsonline.com/articles/tool-path-strategies-for-high-speed-machining).

### High-feed milling -- steer the cutting force into the spindle
- **The technique:** a high-feed end mill has a shallow lead-angle / curved bottom profile that runs at a very LIGHT axial depth of cut but extreme feed. The shallow lead angle "redirects most cutting force vertically along the tool's center axis rather than pushing radially outward." (Confirmed.)
- **WHEN the expert uses it:** roughing in UNSTABLE conditions -- long-reach tools, deep pockets, thin walls -- where a normal radial-force cut would deflect the tool or excite chatter. Because the force points axially "into the spindle instead of radially, there's much less radial force pushing the cutter off its center axis," so "this stability enables longer tool reaches and more aggressive cuts without chatter-induced failure."
- **Why it earns the name:** the curved bottom edge produces a chip-thinning effect, so to avoid rubbing (heat + wear) "feed rates must increase significantly -- hence the name High Feed Mill."
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** trade axial DEPTH (kept very light) for FEED and for force DIRECTION (axial, stabilizing). Cost: it leaves scallops on curved surfaces, has awkward chip geometry in true slotting, and needs CAM that can approximate its radius. It is a roughing/reach strategy, not a finishing one.
- **This PRISM galaxy applies it:** when the mill galaxy detects a long-reach or low-rigidity setup, high-feed is the strategy that keeps the cut stable -- the axial-force direction is the same stability principle as the small entering-angle fact in `mill-foundations.md`, applied at the strategy level.
- Source: [Harvey Performance / In The Loupe -- The Secret Mechanics of High Feed End Mills](https://www.harveyperformance.com/in-the-loupe/the-secret-mechanics-of-high-feed-end-mills/).

## Theme B -- Choosing the right strategy (the decisions an expert makes BEFORE programming)

### HEM vs HSM -- roughing-hog versus finishing-contour, do not confuse them
- **The decision:** High Efficiency Milling (HEM) and High Speed Machining (HSM) are NOT the same thing and are not interchangeable. HSM "employs constant values for the key parameters" with very high spindle speed and MINIMAL axial depth, "optimized for finishing and 3D contouring work" where the tool follows the curved surfaces of the part. HEM, by contrast, is the aggressive STOCK-REMOVAL strategy of Theme A. (Confirmed.)
- **WHEN the expert picks each:** "HSM excels at contouring and finishing applications where light, controlled cuts and high speeds create desired surface geometry"; "HEM succeeds during material hogging phases where aggressive ... engagement and sustained tool usage take priority." The fundamental trade-off is "HSM prioritizes precision and speed through controlled minimal contact, while HEM prioritizes aggressive stock removal and tool efficiency."
- **This PRISM galaxy applies it:** the mill strategy layer should route a roughing/pocketing request to a HEM-family path and a 3D-surface-finish request to an HSM light-contour path -- choosing the wrong one wastes either tool life (over-aggressive finishing) or cycle time (timid roughing).
- Source: [Harvey Performance / In The Loupe -- High Speed Machining vs. HEM](https://www.harveyperformance.com/in-the-loupe/high-efficiency-milling-vs-high-speed-machining/).

### 3+2 positional vs full 5-axis simultaneous -- the rigidity-vs-freeform call
- **The decision:** in 3+2 (positional) machining the two rotary axes index to a fixed angle and LOCK, then ordinary 3-axis cutting runs on that angled face; in full 5-axis simultaneous machining all five axes move continuously, keeping "continuous contact between the tool and work piece" with a dynamically changing tool vector. (Confirmed.)
- **WHEN the expert picks 3+2:** prismatic / multi-face parts and aggressive roughing of steep walls and cavities. Locking the rotaries lets you "use a shorter, more rigid cutting tool than a regular three-axis machining, resulting in greater dimensional stability" and angle that short tool toward the surface for faster feeds/speeds and to reach undercuts. The locked structure is stiffer than a tilting head, so 3+2 is the rigidity choice for heavy cuts.
- **WHEN the expert picks full 5-axis simultaneous:** genuinely freeform / complex curved surfaces (blades, flow paths, organic molds) and undercuts where the tool ORIENTATION must change DURING the cut, and "done-in-one" parts where multiple faces share one datum and re-clamping would stack error. Its rigidity argument is different -- it keeps a short tool at the optimal approach angle even in deep features.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** 3+2 = more rigidity + simpler programming + more index/re-position moves; full 5-axis = continuous-orientation capability + single-setup accuracy at the cost of programming complexity and (often) reduced spindle rigidity while tilting. Default to 3+2 for prismatic/roughing; escalate to simultaneous only when the geometry truly demands a continuously changing tool vector.
- **This PRISM galaxy applies it:** the mill galaxy's setup/strategy planner should NOT reach for simultaneous 5-axis just because the machine has it -- index-and-lock 3+2 is the more rigid, simpler, lower-risk default for prismatic features, reserving simultaneous motion for freeform/undercut geometry.
- Source: [ManufacturingTomorrow -- 3+2 Vs. 5-axis: What's the Difference?](https://www.manufacturingtomorrow.com/article/2019/09/32-vs-5-axis-whats-the-difference/14074).

## Theme C -- Sequencing strategy (logical order is itself the technique)

### Rest machining / re-roughing -- big tool first, then stock-aware smaller tools
- **The technique:** rest machining (re-roughing) is a STOCK-AWARE strategy: a large tool removes the bulk quickly, then the CAM system uses knowledge of exactly where stock REMAINS to drive a smaller tool ONLY into those leftover regions, instead of re-cutting the whole part. "Use a large tool to remove a bulk of the material while generating a second operation with a smaller tool removing any of the remaining material." (Confirmed.)
- **WHEN / WHY the logical order:** this is R13 logical-order discipline expressed in metalcutting. A larger tool clears accessible bulk at the highest MRR; subsequent progressively-smaller rest tools "then address confined zones and corners the initial tool geometry cannot reach -- areas the larger tool necessarily left untouched due to its radius constraints." You never run the small (slow, fragile) tool through already-cleared air.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** "smaller rest tools sacrifice material removal rate (slower cycle time per pass) but gain geometric access to internal features and tight corners." Tool sizing is itself a balance -- too small per step means many slow iterations; reusing the same large size means excessive programming/air-cutting. The expert sizes each rest tool to clear the remaining stock with the fewest iterations.
- **This PRISM galaxy applies it:** the mill galaxy's multi-tool roughing plan should always sequence bulk-removal-then-rest in DEPENDENCY order, carrying a stock model forward so each smaller tool only sees the stock the previous one left -- never re-roughing cleared regions, and never building a finishing pass atop an unproven rough.
- Source: [Hawk Ridge Systems -- Explaining Rest Machining in SOLIDWORKS CAM](https://hawkridgesys.com/blog/explaining-rest-machining-in-solidworks-cam).

## Theme D -- Thin-wall deflection-compensation strategy (the CAM-level move, beyond the bench tactics)

> The applied-practice sibling already covers the BENCH tactics for thin walls (leave support stock, light final passes, climb the finish, wax/thermoplastic damping). This section captures the higher-level CAM-TOOLPATH and force-PREDICTION strategy a world-class programmer plans BEFORE the part is on the machine.

### Waterline (constant-Z) cut pattern + force-prediction -- keep the workpiece rigid as long as possible
- **The technique:** a thin wall loses stiffness AS it is machined, so the governing strategy is to keep the not-yet-cut workpiece as rigid as possible for as long as possible, and to choose the cutter-PATH PATTERN that produces the least force-induced form error. A peer-reviewed numerical study found the WATERLINE pattern -- step down in constant Z-levels, ALTERNATING sides at each level -- "produces the least surface error", beating a side-by-side pattern (which finishes one whole side before switching) on maximum error, average error, AND standard deviation. (Confirmed.)
- **WHY it works:** "during material removal, the stiffness of the workpiece becomes even lower which aggravate[s] the in-process deflections." Alternating sides at each Z-step preserves the workpiece stiffness because the thick unmachined bulk below the cut keeps supporting the fragile free edge longer than a one-side-to-the-floor pass would.
- **WHEN the expert uses force-prediction:** wall deflection "is governed mainly by the selection of cutter path strategies" and is driven by the cutting FORCE; the state of the art is a model that "continuously update[s]" the workpiece geometry and recomputes the reduced stiffness at the engagement zone to PREDICT the resulting thickness error per strategy -- so the programmer picks the path BEFORE cutting metal rather than discovering taper on the CMM.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** the waterline strategy "trades slightly increased non-cutting time for significantly improved geometric accuracy." A larger machining allowance left for the finish pass yields smaller thickness errors; a larger axial depth lowers the maximum error but raises the average -- so axial depth on the final wall passes is a deliberate accuracy lever, kept light. (The allowance and depth NUMBERS are owner-gated.)
- **This PRISM galaxy applies it:** when the mill galaxy plans a thin-wall feature it should emit an alternating-side waterline finish (not one-side-to-the-floor), keep the wall stock until the last low-engagement passes, and -- where available -- gate the plan on a force/deflection prediction rather than a fixed feed.
- Source: [Scientific Reports / PubMed Central (open access) -- Numerical evaluation of cutting strategies for thin-walled parts](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10791679/) (peer-reviewed open-access research).
