## Coolant strategy as a cutting variable (not just lubrication)

The advanced shop treats coolant *delivery* as a tuning lever, not a fixed background utility.

- **High-pressure, precisely-aimed coolant turns coolant into an active chip-control and heat tool.** Conventional flood pumps fluid indiscriminately into the general work area; a high-pressure jet concentrated exactly at the tool-chip interface does three things flood cannot: it removes far more heat per unit of fluid by hitting the shear zone where friction generates it, it actively flushes chips away from the cut (preventing chip re-engagement and re-cutting), and it helps fracture an otherwise-continuous chip into short manageable segments. The expert reaches for it on the materials that defeat flood -- ductile/gummy alloys and difficult-to-machine grades that produce long stringy chips, and deep narrow cuts (grooving/parting) where flood cannot reach the bottom of the slot. The trade-off direction: HPC adds pump/plumbing/rotary-union cost and machine complexity, and is bought back in chip control, heat removal, and tool life. ([Wikipedia -- Cutting fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cutting_fluid))
- **Aim beats volume -- targeting the cutting zone is the strategy, not flooding harder.** The same source makes the strategic point explicit: flood "works everywhere indiscriminately," whereas targeted delivery "works only where needed," maximizing thermal and lubricating effectiveness at the critical interface. The lubrication role (reducing tool-chip friction) is preserved while the cooling role is sharpened. The expert's move when chips weld to the edge or won't break is to *re-aim and pressurize the coolant at the edge*, before changing the cut. ([Wikipedia -- Cutting fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cutting_fluid))
- *PRISM application:* the lathe galaxy's chip-control and unattended-turning reasoning should model coolant *delivery mode* (flood vs targeted vs high-pressure) as a strategy input alongside feed/chipbreaker -- pairing the foundations chip-form theory with a coolant-strategy lever -- while every psi value stays owner-gated in `constants.ts`.

## Constant-surface-speed as a deliberate G96/G97/clamp strategy

Foundations defines G96 vs G97; the *advanced* layer is treating the choice -- and the speed clamp -- as a strategic and safety decision, not a default.

- **Use G96 selectively where diameter swings, not blanket-wide.** CSS earns its keep precisely when "the beginning and end of a cut will differ significantly in diameter" -- facing across to center, large-range roughing, taper passes -- because there it holds cutting conditions roughly constant across the radius. On a part of nearly-uniform diameter G96 buys little, so the expert does not reach for it reflexively. ([CNCCookbook -- G96 Constant Surface Speed](https://www.cnccookbook.com/g96-g-code-constant-surface-speed-cnc/))
- **The maximum-spindle-speed clamp is a mandatory safety partner to CSS, not an optional nicety.** Under G96 the commanded RPM rises as the cutting diameter shrinks; without a max-RPM clamp the spindle "will take off to its highest allowed rpm" as the tool approaches center, creating balance problems and dangerous centrifugal loading on the workpiece and chuck. The expert always pairs CSS with the max-RPM clamp so the runaway is bounded -- the clamp is the thing that makes CSS safe near center. The promoted rule is the *direction and necessity* (smaller diameter -> higher commanded RPM -> clamp required); the specific cap value is owner-gated. ([CNCCookbook -- G96 Constant Surface Speed](https://www.cnccookbook.com/g96-g-code-constant-surface-speed-cnc/))
- **Switch to G97 (fixed RPM) for on-center and synchronized work.** The expert deliberately drops CSS for drilling/tapping/reaming on the center axis (where CSS would command max RPM no matter the surface speed asked), for threading (where spindle synchronization, not surface speed, is what must be held), and as the cut nears center where surface speed collapses. The trade-off direction: G96 gives consistent cutting conditions across diameter at the cost of vigilance (and a mandatory clamp); G97 gives predictable, synchronizable spindle behavior. ([CNCCookbook -- G96 Constant Surface Speed](https://www.cnccookbook.com/g96-g-code-constant-surface-speed-cnc/))
- *PRISM application:* the galaxy's program-generation should choose G96/G97 per operation (CSS for facing/roughing with large diameter swing, G97 for center-drilling/threading), and emit the max-RPM clamp whenever CSS is active -- with the clamp value drawn from `constants.ts`, never hard-coded in a post.

## Done-in-one: mill-turn live tooling, multi-axis, and complete machining

The single highest-leverage modern turning strategy is collapsing a multi-setup part into one machine cycle.

- **Adding live tooling and extra axes (Y/B, rotary) lets one machine do what once took several setups -- and the accuracy gain comes from the eliminated re-fixturing.** The strategic payoff is not just convenience: "what once required multiple setups now can be executed in only a few if not one, reducing steps and decreasing the opportunity for error." Each re-fixturing introduces a fresh datum and a fresh chance for misalignment; doing turning *and* the milled/drilled features in one clamping removes those compounding errors, so concentricity and dimensional accuracy improve because the part is never re-referenced. Extra axes also enable more complex geometry (e.g. curved/off-axis holes) and let the tool meet the surface at a better angle, extending tool life. ([Wikipedia -- Multiaxis machining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiaxis_machining))
- **The trade-off is machine and programming complexity, and CAM is the binding constraint.** Multi-axis turn-mill capability comes "at the cost of increased complexity and price of the machine," and the source names CAM software as "the limiting factor" -- i.e. the bottleneck on a done-in-one strategy is usually the programming/CAM side, not the iron. The expert weighs the eliminated-setup accuracy/throughput gain against that complexity, and reaches for done-in-one most aggressively on parts with tight feature-to-feature concentricity and many secondary (milled/drilled) features. ([Wikipedia -- Multiaxis machining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiaxis_machining))
- *PRISM application:* the lathe galaxy should flag candidate parts for mill-turn done-in-one when a print carries off-axis/milled/cross-drilled features *plus* tight concentricity callouts between turned and milled features, and route the harder problem to the CAM/post layer (the named limiting factor) rather than to the cutting-data layer.

## Hard turning vs grinding -- the process-selection decision

Choosing turning over grinding on hardened material is a top-of-field decision an expert makes from the part's requirements.

- **Hard turning (machining hardened steel with CBN/ceramic tooling) can replace grinding when setup flexibility and stock removal beat ultimate finish.** Hard turning machines already-hardened workpieces, and its strategic advantage over grinding is that complex contours and multiple features can be completed "in one setup," with flexibility in tool and geometry choice -- whereas grinding often needs multiple operations and dedicated wheels. It is "generally better than grinding for rough stock removal" on hard parts. ([Wikipedia -- Hard turning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_turning))
- **Grinding still wins where ultimate surface finish and dimensional accuracy are the priority.** The same source is explicit that grinding "generally produces a better surface finish and can produce parts with greater dimensional accuracy." So the expert decision is a clean trade-off *direction*: hard turning for throughput, single-setup completion of complex/contoured hardened parts, and rough stock removal; grinding when the finish/accuracy callout is the dominant requirement. The two are often sequenced (hard-turn to near-net, grind the critical surface). ([Wikipedia -- Hard turning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_turning))
- *PRISM application:* when a part is hardened above the gated hardness threshold, the galaxy should surface a process-selection branch (hard-turn vs grind vs hard-turn-then-grind) driven by the print's surface-finish and tolerance callouts -- the qualitative branch is promoted here; the hardness threshold, the achievable-finish numbers, and CBN cutting data stay owner-gated.

## Multi-pass single-point threading -- the depth-schedule strategy

Applied-practice covers the *infeed-method-vs-chatter* choice; the advanced layer is the multi-pass *depth schedule* that holds cutting load constant across the thread.

- **A thread is cut in progressively shallower passes to keep the cutting load bounded, not by removing equal depth each pass.** Single-point threading uses multiple light cuts to reach full depth. The strategy reason depth must *decrease* on later passes: as the V deepens, the tool's flank engagement (the cut cross-section / chip width) grows pass-over-pass, so holding a constant infeed would steadily raise cutting force, friction, and heat. By shrinking the infeed on successive passes, the expert holds the per-pass cutting area roughly constant and keeps forces and temperature manageable to the last pass. ([Wikipedia -- Threading (manufacturing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threading_(manufacturing)))
- **Spindle-synchronized chasing is what makes repeatable multi-pass threading possible.** Each pass must re-enter the existing groove on lead, which requires the tool motion to be locked to spindle rotation -- "the precise rotation of the workpiece determines the lead of the thread," and modern CNC maintains a constant spindle-tool relationship so successive passes track the same helix. This is the strategic reason threading runs in fixed-RPM mode (cross-ref [foundations](lathe-foundations.md#threading-must-run-in-fixed-rpm-g97-mode)) and why a light finishing/spring pass at final depth (cutting little or nothing) is used to clean up the flanks after the load-bearing passes. ([Wikipedia -- Threading (manufacturing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threading_(manufacturing)))
- *PRISM application:* the galaxy's thread-cycle generation should emit a *decreasing* per-pass infeed schedule (constant-cutting-area shape) plus spindle synchronization and finishing pass(es), choosing the infeed *method* per the applied-practice rule -- with the actual pass count, per-pass depths, and infeed angle all owner-gated in `constants.ts`.

## Automation strategy -- bar feeding, Swiss-type, and lights-out turning

At the top of the field the strategy extends past the single cut to how parts flow through the machine unattended.

- **Bar feeding converts a lathe into a continuous, near-unattended part producer.** A bar feeder pushes/pulls bar stock through the spindle so that, once a part is cut off, "the chuck is released, the bar fed forward, and the chuck closed again, ready for the next cycle" -- with advancement by pulling-finger or roller-pusher mechanisms. The strategic payoff: one operator can "monitor the operation of several machines," and for high-volume turned components "nothing is as cost-efficient" as a properly set-up bar-fed automatic. The trade-off direction: heavy up-front setup effort, repaid by sustained unattended throughput -- so the expert reaches for it on volume work, not one-offs. ([Wikipedia -- Automatic lathe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automatic_lathe))
- **Swiss-type turning is the strategy of choice for long, slender, high-precision bar parts.** A Swiss-type lathe slides the headstock so the bar is supported by a guide bushing *immediately adjacent to the cutting tool*, with the tools stationary in Z and the material moving past them -- so "all the work is done on the material near the guide bushing where it is more rigid." This collapses the unsupported overhang to almost nothing right at the cut, defeating the slender-part deflection/whip that an ordinary lathe fights with steady/follower rests (cross-ref [applied-practice](lathe-applied-practice.md#setup--fixturing-gotchas)). Combined with a sub-spindle that catches the cut-off part for back-side operations and ejects it, it is a complete unattended cell -- the reason it dominates medical, watch, and connector-pin work needing extreme accuracy. The trade-off: guide-bushing setup and maintenance complexity, and a fixed bar-end remnant, repaid by deflection-free precision on high-L/D parts at high volume. ([Wikipedia -- Swiss lathe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_lathe))
- *PRISM application:* the galaxy should route part candidates by automation strategy -- bar-fed automatic for high-volume turned stock, Swiss-type for high-aspect-ratio precision bar parts where conventional support can't keep up -- and treat the guide-bushing-near-the-cut rigidity advantage as the structural alternative to the steady/follower-rest gotcha in the applied-practice layer. Max bar diameter/length limits and tolerance figures stay owner-gated.
