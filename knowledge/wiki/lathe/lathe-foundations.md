## Constant surface speed (CSS) — the G96 / G97 relationship

- **G96 = Constant Surface Speed; G97 = fixed/constant RPM.** Under G96 the control automatically adjusts spindle RPM as the cutting diameter changes so that the surface speed at the cutting point stays constant; `G97` cancels CSS and returns the spindle to plain RPM mode. ([CNCCookbook — G96 Constant Surface Speed](https://www.cnccookbook.com/g96-g-code-constant-surface-speed-cnc/))
- **Qualitative CSS behavior — RPM rises as diameter shrinks.** A vendor control example shows the commanded spindle speed climbing (e.g. roughly 3,185 RPM up toward a 12,000 RPM cap) as the tool moves from a large X toward the spindle centerline; i.e. smaller diameter -> higher RPM at fixed surface speed. This is the qualitative motivation for the `G50 S<max-rpm>` cap. ([Masso Docs — G96 Turn on Constant Surface Speed](https://docs.masso.com.au/supported-g-codes/g96-turn-on-constant-surface-speed-css))
- **CSS / RPM formula STRUCTURE (geometry only).** Cutting speed relates to diameter and spindle speed by `vc = (pi x Dm x n) / 1000` (metric, Dm in mm, vc in m/min) and `vc = (pi x Dm x n) / 12` (imperial). Inverting gives the spindle-speed-from-surface-speed form `n = (1000 x vc) / (pi x Dm)` (metric) / `n = (12 x vc) / (pi x Dm)` (imperial). This is pure geometry, not a cutting constant. ([Mitsubishi Materials — Formula for Turning](https://www.mmc-carbide.com/us/technical_information/formula/tec_turning_formula))

## Feed on a lathe is per-revolution

- **Lathe feed is natively per spindle revolution (IPR / mm-per-rev), not per minute.** Because the workpiece rotates in turning and boring, feed units are "almost always distance per spindle revolution (inches per revolution [ipr] or millimeters per revolution [mm/rev])"; milling (stationary workpiece) uses distance-per-time instead. ([Wikipedia — Speeds and feeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speeds_and_feeds))
- **Feed-rate relationship STRUCTURE.** Feed per revolution relates to the per-minute feed and spindle speed by `f = l / n` (feed-per-rev = cutting-length-per-minute / spindle-speed), i.e. per-minute feed = feed-per-rev x spindle-speed. Structure only. ([Mitsubishi Materials — Formula for Turning](https://www.mmc-carbide.com/us/technical_information/formula/tec_turning_formula))

## Theoretical surface finish from feed and nose radius

- **Peak roughness scales as feed-squared over nose radius.** The theoretical maximum surface roughness for turning with a rounded-nose tool is `h_max = f^2 / (8 x r)`, where f = feed per revolution and r = tool nose radius. The load-bearing fact is the STRUCTURE: theoretical finish is proportional to feed-squared and inversely proportional to nose radius (so halving feed roughly quarters the theoretical peak roughness). ([minaprem — Derive surface-roughness formula for turning with a rounded tool](http://www.minaprem.com/machining/principle/quality/derive-formula-for-surface-roughness-in-turning-with-a-rounded-tool/))
- **Independent vendor confirmation of the same structure.** A second primary source presents the identical theoretical-finish structure `h = f^2 / (8 x RE)` (RE = insert corner radius). Two independent sources agreeing on `f^2 / (8 x r)` is what makes this promotable as structure. ([Mitsubishi Materials — Formula for Turning](https://www.mmc-carbide.com/us/technical_information/formula/tec_turning_formula))

> Note on the `f^2 / (32 x r)` form: the staging packet also cited an Ra (centre-line-average) variant with a factor of 32. The sources I fetched confirm only the **Rmax/peak** form (factor 8). The Ra-vs-Rmax factor distinction is left owner-gated for whiskey to reconcile (see Owner-gate below). The promoted, verified claim is the proportionality `finish ~ f^2 / r`, not any specific divisor for Ra.

## Threading must run in fixed-RPM (G97) mode

- **Thread with G97 (fixed RPM), not G96.** For threaded cycles the lead must stay synchronized to spindle revolution; what matters is synchronizing the axis motion to the spindle, not holding an exact surface speed over the small tip-to-trough diameter change. CSS RPM variation would break that pitch synchronization, so "many controls require rpm mode during threading." ([CNCCookbook — G96 Constant Surface Speed](https://www.cnccookbook.com/g96-g-code-constant-surface-speed-cnc/))

## Lathe anatomy — the component spine of every turning machine

- **The lathe's functional components and what each does.** The **headstock** houses high-precision spinning bearings and the spindle that rotates the workpiece, converting motor speed into a range of spindle speeds (cone pulleys or gear trains). The **tailstock** ("loose head") slides along the bed to support long workpieces; its hollow barrel holds a hardened center (to support thin shafts) or a drill bit (for axial holes). The **carriage** (saddle + apron) supports the **cross-slide**, a flat piece that sits crosswise on the bed and cranks at right angles to the bed axis — this produces cuts perpendicular to the spindle axis. The **compound rest** mounts atop the cross-slide and adds two more axes (rotary + linear) for angled/complex cuts. The **lead screw** drives axis motion to control tool advance, and the **bed** is the horizontal foundation supporting all moving components. Knowing which member carries each motion is the prerequisite for any setup, fixturing, or rigidity analysis. ([Wikipedia — Lathe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lathe))

## The six canonical turning operations (qualitative method map)

- **Facing** cuts the workpiece end flat, perpendicular to its axis. **Turning** reduces the outer diameter along the workpiece length. **Parting** separates material by cutting perpendicular all the way through the workpiece. **Boring** enlarges an existing internal hole parallel to the axis. **Knurling** rolls a textured diamond or straight pattern onto the surface. **Threading** produces screw threads via synchronized spindle and lead-screw motion. This operation taxonomy is the qualitative backbone for routing a turned part through its op sequence; the synchronized-motion requirement of threading is the *method-level* reason threading runs in fixed-RPM mode (see [Threading must run in fixed-RPM (G97) mode](#threading-must-run-in-fixed-rpm-g97-mode) above). ([Wikipedia — Lathe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lathe))

## The physics under CSS — tangential speed is angular speed times radius (free textbook)

- **CSS rests on the rigid-body rotation identity `v_t = r * omega`.** A free university-physics text defines angular velocity as the time rate of change of angle (`omega = d(theta)/dt`) and establishes that the tangential (linear) speed of a point is its angular velocity times the radius of its circular path: `v_t = r * omega`. The load-bearing qualitative consequence: for a *fixed* angular velocity (fixed RPM), tangential speed at the cutting edge increases with distance from the rotation axis — so when the cutting diameter changes during a turning pass, the only way to hold surface speed constant is to vary RPM inversely with radius. This is the first-principles justification for G96/CSS that the earlier vendor-sourced CSS section asserts operationally. ([OpenStax — University Physics Volume 1, Sec. 10.1 Rotational Variables](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/10-1-rotational-variables))

## Surface-finish metrology — the parameter family and the standard that governs it (government source)

- **Turning quality is verified against a defined family of profile parameters, not just "Ra."** A U.S. national-metrology standard reference material certifies surface roughness against a parameter set that includes **Ra** (roughness average / arithmetic mean about the profile mean line), **Rq** (root-mean-square roughness), **Rz** (average maximum profile height), **Rt** (maximum profile height), **Rp** (max peak height), **Rv** (max valley depth), and **RSm** (mean spacing of profile irregularities). Knowing that `Ra` is only one of several defined parameters — and that peak/valley parameters (`Rt`, `Rp`, `Rv`) capture worst-case features `Ra` averages away — is essential for reading a print's surface-finish callout correctly. ([NIST SRM 2071b certificate](https://tsapps.nist.gov/srmext/certificates/archives/2071b.pdf))
- **The measurement METHOD: stylus profilometer + Gaussian filter + bounded evaluation length, governed by ASME B46.1.** The certified procedure uses a computerized stylus instrument; a Gaussian-type filter with a defined long-wavelength cutoff separates *roughness* from *waviness*, and the parameters are evaluated over a standardized evaluation length. The Ra definition and the measurement procedure are governed by **ASME B46.1 (Surface Texture — Surface Roughness, Waviness, and Lay)**. The method-level point for PRISM: a finish number is only meaningful with its filter cutoff and evaluation length, and the roughness-vs-waviness split is a filtering choice, not a property of the part. ([NIST SRM 2071b certificate](https://tsapps.nist.gov/srmext/certificates/archives/2071b.pdf))

## Tool-wear mechanisms — the qualitative failure modes behind tool life (government report)

- **Wear debris is produced by distinct, nameable mechanisms.** A NASA technical report on wear microstructure outlines debris-production mechanisms that apply to the rubbing/cutting interface: **extrusion of microfurrows** (wear particles formed by material displacement), **adhesive transfer** (material transferred between contacting surfaces), and **fragmentation** (material breaking into debris). It examines a thin **transfer layer** and the **near-surface deformation zone** beneath the contact, and notes that the **microstructure of dry wear differs from lubricated wear**. This qualitative mechanism vocabulary is what tool-life and coolant-strategy reasoning rests on (it is the *why* behind the owner-gated Taylor tool-life numbers, which stay in `constants.ts`). ([NASA NTRS — Microstructures Associated with Wear (19840015833)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19840015833))

## Spindle drive theory — torque is what a spindle delivers, and its structure (free textbook)

- **Torque is the rotational analog of force, and the spindle's law of motion is `tau_net = I * alpha`.** A free university-physics text establishes Newton's second law for rotation: the net torque on a body equals its moment of inertia times its angular acceleration, `tau_net = I * alpha` — structurally parallel to `sum(F) = m * a` for linear motion, with moment of inertia `I` playing the role of mass and angular acceleration `alpha` the role of linear acceleration. The method-level consequence for a turning machine: the spindle motor delivers *torque*, not raw force, so a heavy/large-diameter workpiece (large `I`) accelerates rotationally more slowly under a given motor torque — the qualitative basis for spin-up time, rigid-tapping sync, and why low-RPM high-diameter cuts are torque-limited rather than power-limited. (Power vs torque numbers stay owner-gated.) ([OpenStax — University Physics Volume 1, Sec. 10.7 Newton's Second Law for Rotation](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/10-7-newtons-second-law-for-rotation))
- **Torque STRUCTURE: force times lever arm, maximized perpendicular.** The same text defines torque as depending on the applied-force magnitude, the lever (moment) arm `r` = perpendicular distance from the rotation axis to the line of action, and the angle of application — with maximum torque (`tau = r*F`) when the force acts perpendicular to the lever arm. For lathe reasoning this is the geometry behind cutting-torque-at-radius: a tangential cutting force applied at the work radius produces a torque scaling with that radius, so the spindle torque demand of a cut grows with cutting diameter at fixed cutting force. Structure/geometry only — no force or torque values. ([OpenStax — University Physics Volume 1, Sec. 10.7 Newton's Second Law for Rotation](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/10-7-newtons-second-law-for-rotation))

## Rotational inertia — why workpiece mass distribution governs spin-up and chuck loading (free textbook)

- **Moment of inertia is `I = sum(m_j * r_j^2)` (discrete) / `I = integral(r^2 dm)` (continuous) — mass farther from the axis counts quadratically.** A free university-physics text defines rotational inertia as the mass-weighted sum/integral of squared distance from the rotation axis, so mass located farther from the spindle centerline contributes disproportionately (squared-distance term). Qualitatively: a large-OD or unevenly-distributed workpiece has far more rotational inertia than its bulk mass alone suggests — directly relevant to spin-up/braking time, out-of-balance loads on the chuck and bearings, and why thin-wall large-diameter parts are inertia-dominated. Structure + mechanism only. ([OpenStax — University Physics Volume 1, Sec. 10.5 Calculating Moments of Inertia](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/10-5-calculating-moments-of-inertia))
- **Parallel-axis theorem STRUCTURE: `I_axis = I_cm + m*d^2`.** The text gives the parallel-axis theorem — the moment of inertia about any axis equals the moment of inertia about a parallel axis through the center of mass plus the mass times the squared offset distance `d`. The method-level point for workholding: an *eccentrically* clamped or off-center workpiece carries additional rotational inertia (and therefore additional out-of-balance dynamic loading) that grows with the square of the offset from the spindle axis — the qualitative justification for centering/balancing before high-RPM turning. Geometry only — no numbers. ([OpenStax — University Physics Volume 1, Sec. 10.5 Calculating Moments of Inertia](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/10-5-calculating-moments-of-inertia))

## Lathe hazard taxonomy — what makes a turning machine dangerous (government safety source)

- **A turning machine carries three classes of mechanical hazard: cutting action, rotating parts, and in-running nip points.** OSHA's Machine Guarding eTool classifies cutting-action hazards as involving rotating, reciprocating, or transverse motion, with injury risk concentrated at the **point of operation** — and it explicitly lists **turning machines (lathes)** among the mechanisms where "flying chips or scrap material can strike the head, particularly in the area of the eyes or face." Separately, even a smooth slowly-rotating shaft can grip hair or clothing and force a hand/arm into a dangerous position; hazardous rotating members named include couplings, cams, flywheels, shafts, and gears, worsened by protruding set screws or bolts. The qualitative method takeaway for PRISM safety reasoning: chip-ejection eye/face hazard and rotating-member entanglement are *intrinsic* to turning and must be guarded at the point of operation, independent of any cutting numbers. ([OSHA eTool — Machine Guarding: Hazardous Motions and Actions](https://www.osha.gov/etools/machine-guarding/introduction/hazardous-motions-actions))
- **In-running nip points are a named, enumerable hazard class.** The same source enumerates three in-running nip-point categories: (1) oppositely-rotating parts on parallel axes (intermeshing gears, rolling mills), (2) a rotating part tangent to a moving component (belt/pulley, chain/sprocket), and (3) a rotating part shearing/crushing against a fixed part (spoked wheels, screw conveyors, grinder wheels). For a lathe this frames where guards belong on power-transmission and feed members, not just the cutting zone. Qualitative hazard taxonomy only. ([OSHA eTool — Machine Guarding: Hazardous Motions and Actions](https://www.osha.gov/etools/machine-guarding/introduction/hazardous-motions-actions))

## Lathe safety standard — the consensus standard OSHA points to (government source)

- **Lathe safeguarding is governed by the consensus standard ANSI B11.6 within the ANSI B11 series.** OSHA's Machine Guarding eTool standards page lists **ANSI B11.6 — "Lathes"** in the ANSI B11 series of machine-safety consensus standards (turning machines), and frames lathe safety within the general machine-guarding regulations at **29 CFR 1910.211–1910.219**, noting OSHA may cite violations by referencing the ANSI B11 series. The method-level point: PRISM's lathe-safety gates should treat ANSI B11.6 (lathes) + 29 CFR 1910.211–1910.219 (general machine guarding) as the governing standards framing — a standards pointer, not a numeric requirement. ([OSHA eTool — Machine Guarding: Standards](https://www.osha.gov/etools/machine-guarding/standards))
