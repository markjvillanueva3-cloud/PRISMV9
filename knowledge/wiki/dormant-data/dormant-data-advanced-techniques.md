## 1. Collect while the system runs — concurrent + incremental tracing

### Technique 1.1 — Run the collector concurrently with the mutator, not stop-the-world
The foundations entry introduced stop-the-world mark-sweep. The advanced move is to NOT halt the workload: "Concurrent garbage collectors do not stop program execution at all, except perhaps briefly when the program's execution stack is scanned" [Tracing garbage collection, Wikipedia — confirmed]. Incremental, concurrent, and real-time collectors "address these problems, with varying trade-offs" [Garbage collection (computer science), Wikipedia — confirmed].
- **WHEN an expert uses it:** when the reclaimer cannot be allowed to freeze the live workload — i.e. always, in a system with continuously-active consumers.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** lower pause/latency (the workload keeps running) is bought with lower total throughput and more memory headroom — "the sum of the incremental phases takes longer to complete than one batch garbage collection pass, so these garbage collectors may yield lower total throughput" [Tracing GC — confirmed]. You trade total CPU/time for the workload never stalling.
- **Galaxy application:** the dormant-data sweep over the 644 MB graph / hundred-MB indexes must run *concurrently and in phases* while 26 slots are live, never as one stop-the-world walk that starves them. Classify-then-reclaim in incremental passes; accept that the full sweep takes longer wall-clock in exchange for never freezing the fleet (this is the applied-practice throttling concern stated as a positive design strategy).

### Technique 1.2 — Use a write barrier to keep the tri-color invariant true under concurrent mutation
Concurrent collection is only safe if the mutator can't break the tri-color invariant ("No black objects reference white objects... This is called the tri-color invariant" [Tri-color marking, Wikipedia — confirmed]) while it keeps mutating the graph. The mechanism is a **write barrier**: "a fragment of code emitted by the compiler immediately before every store operation to ensure that (e.g.) generational invariants are maintained" [Write barrier, Wikipedia — confirmed]. On every pointer write the barrier records the affected edge so the collector re-examines it, preventing a live (white) object from being hidden behind an already-scanned (black) one and wrongly reclaimed.
- **WHEN an expert uses it:** any time the data graph is being EDITED while the orphan/reachability scan is in flight — unavoidable on a live fleet.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** a small fixed cost on every mutation (the barrier fires on writes, not reads) buys correctness during concurrent collection. You pay per-write overhead to avoid stopping the world.
- **Galaxy application:** the foundations §5 tri-color invariant ("don't reap mid-write") becomes operational here. A dormant-data orphan audit that runs while peers add/remove graph edges must record edges created *during* the scan (a barrier-equivalent: re-check anything written since the mark started) — otherwise a peer wiring a fresh consumer to a "white" artifact mid-scan gets it reaped. The barrier is the strategy that makes a concurrent orphan audit safe instead of a race.

### Technique 1.3 — Accept floating garbage as the price of not stopping; sweep it next cycle
A concurrent collector that started its mark before an object died will conservatively keep that object this cycle (it was reachable at the snapshot instant). This "floating garbage" — dead-but-survived-this-pass — is the deliberate, correct cost of snapshot-consistent concurrent collection (the snapshot-at-the-beginning discipline: collect what was unreachable when the cycle began; catch the rest next cycle). [Derived from the tri-color invariant + concurrent-collection trade-off above; the precise term "floating garbage" is standard GC literature and is treated here only as the *direction* of the trade-off, not a numeric retention claim.]
- **WHEN an expert uses it:** whenever choosing concurrency over precision — accept a bounded lag between "became dead" and "got reclaimed."
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** more transient space retained (floating garbage lingers one cycle) buys a simpler, race-free concurrent invariant. Precision is traded for safety + concurrency.
- **Galaxy application:** the ledger should NOT chase reaping an artifact the instant it becomes orphaned mid-sweep — let it float to the next pass when its deadness is unambiguous. This directly defuses the premature-reclaim / dangling-read class (applied-practice §2): a one-cycle delay is a cheap insurance premium against freeing something a concurrent reader still holds.

## 2. Don't scan what you don't have to — generational + incremental skipping

### Technique 2.1 — Generational collection: collect the young set often, the old set rarely, via a remembered set
The generational hypothesis ("most objects die young") motivates separating data by age: "two or more allocation regions (generations) are kept... separate based on the object's age. New objects are created in the 'young' generation that is regularly collected, and when a generation is full, the objects that are still referenced from older regions are copied into the next oldest generation" [Garbage collection (computer science), Wikipedia — confirmed]. The enabling trick for collecting the young set *without* rescanning the entire old set: track old-to-young references in a **remembered set** — "The objects in the older generation that may reference objects in new space are kept in a 'remembered set'" [Tracing garbage collection, Wikipedia — confirmed] — and treat those entries as extra roots for the minor collection.
- **WHEN an expert uses it:** when most dead data concentrates among recently-created short-lived artifacts (the common case) and full corpus scans are expensive.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** a frequent cheap young-set pass + a remembered-set bookkeeping cost buys you NOT having to scan the whole old generation every collection. Per-write remembered-set maintenance is traded for vastly cheaper minor collections.
- **Galaxy application:** foundations §6 said "look for dead data among recently-created artifacts first"; this is the *mechanism*. The dormant-data sweep should scan the young set (tmp files, staging dirs, one-shot ledgers — where deadness concentrates) frequently and cheaply, and scan long-lived stable corpora rarely — while maintaining a "remembered set" of the few stable corpora that point INTO young artifacts, so a frequent young-sweep never misses a live young node pinned by an old one. This makes the per-pass cost scale with churn, not with total corpus size.

### Technique 2.2 — Maintain a visibility/clean-page map so reclamation skips provably-settled regions
PostgreSQL's production answer to "don't re-scan settled data" is the visibility map: "Vacuum maintains a visibility map for each table to keep track of which pages contain only tuples that are known to be visible to all active transactions... vacuum itself can skip such pages on the next run, since there is nothing to clean up" [PostgreSQL routine vacuuming — confirmed].
- **WHEN an expert uses it:** when the dataset is large and mostly stable, so re-scanning unchanged regions every pass dominates cost.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** a small per-region "is this fully settled?" bit buys skipping clean regions entirely on the next pass. A little metadata is traded for large reductions in scan I/O on big, stable corpora.
- **Galaxy application:** the dormant-data reclaimer should carry a per-region "clean since last sweep + no in-flight consumer" marker (analogous to the visibility map) so a sweep over the multi-hundred-MB indexes touches only regions that changed since last pass — turning a full O(corpus) walk into O(churn). Pairs with the offset-indexed sidecar pattern the fleet already uses (cheap-node-access seekable cards).

## 3. Prove no live snapshot references it — MVCC + RCU snapshot-safe reclaim

### Technique 3.1 — Gate version reclamation on the OLDEST active snapshot, not on "logically deleted"
MVCC keeps multiple versions so readers see a consistent snapshot without blocking writers ("each user connected to the database sees a snapshot of the database at a particular instant in time... Read and write transactions are thus isolated from each other without any need for locking" [Multiversion concurrency control, Wikipedia — confirmed]). The advanced reclaim rule is that a superseded version is reclaimable ONLY once no active snapshot could still need it: "MVCC introduces the challenge of how to remove versions that become obsolete and will never be read" — e.g. "version 0 of Object 1 can be garbage collected" only after "all transactions that start after T1 commits" [same source — confirmed]. The reclaim gate is the oldest still-needed read timestamp.
- **WHEN an expert uses it:** whenever readers and a reclaimer run concurrently against versioned data — the only safe ordering.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** holding obsolete versions until the oldest snapshot advances costs transient space but guarantees no concurrent reader is corrupted. Space-now is traded for read-correctness — and a long-lived reader (a stuck transaction) can pin a lot of dead versions, which is the cost direction to watch.
- **Galaxy application:** this is the applied-practice premature-reclaim gotcha turned into a positive strategy. The dormant-data reaper must compute the equivalent of an "oldest active snapshot" across the fleet — the earliest still-open consumer of an artifact version — and refuse to free anything newer than that watermark. Edge-count + age never authorize a free; the oldest-live-reader watermark does.

### Technique 3.2 — RCU grace periods: defer the free until every pre-existing reader has demonstrably finished
Read-copy-update is the lock-free version of the same idea, and it is the world-leader pattern for reclamation under heavy concurrent reads: readers run with no locks while a writer publishes a new version, and "Once a grace period has elapsed, there can no longer be any readers referencing the old version, so it is then safe for the reclamation phase to free (reclaim) the data items" — where a grace period is "any time period during which each thread resides at least once in a quiescent state" [Read-copy-update, Wikipedia — confirmed]. "The ability to wait until all readers are done allows RCU readers to use much lighter-weight synchronization—in some cases, no synchronization at all" [same source — confirmed].
- **WHEN an expert uses it:** read-mostly data with many concurrent readers you must not block, where you can detect when all in-flight readers have moved on.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** deferring the free until a grace period elapses costs a bounded delay + transient memory (old + new version coexist) but makes readers completely non-blocking ("RCU is completely wait-free for any number of readers"). Reader speed is bought with delayed reclamation.
- **Galaxy application:** the safest fleet reclaimer publishes a NEW artifact (atomic rename / new sidecar), lets the old one stay readable, and frees the old copy only after a "grace period" — i.e. after every chat that may have opened the old version has demonstrably finished (no open FD, no in-flight read; the applied-practice `/proc/*/fd` + identity-recheck discipline is the quiescence detector). This is the structural fix for the fail-OPEN clobber (`a3e6d3ca9`): never overwrite-in-place a corpus a reader holds; copy-forward, publish, and reclaim the old version only after the grace period proves no reader dangles.

## 4. Reclaim by rewriting, not deleting in place — log-structured compaction strategy

### Technique 4.1 — Buffer writes, flush immutable runs, reclaim dead data by COMPACTION (tombstones + merge)
The modern storage-lifecycle engine never overwrites in place: it buffers in memory, flushes immutable sorted runs, and reclaims superseded/deleted data during a background merge. "Once the in-memory buffer becomes full, it is flushed to the disk as an immutable sorted component... This flush is performed sequentially, ensuring high I/O efficiency by avoiding the costly random writes." Deletes don't free anything immediately: "Deletes are marked with a tombstone entry, which is a placeholder indicating that the key has been deleted. These tombstones are later removed during the merging process," and "LSM trees employ a merge process similar to merge sort to consolidate entries" [Log-structured merge-tree, Wikipedia — confirmed].
- **WHEN an expert uses it:** write-heavy / append-mostly corpora where in-place mutation is expensive or unsafe, and reclamation can be batched into background compaction.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** deferring reclamation to a background merge buys sequential-write efficiency and crash-safe immutability, at the cost of dead data (tombstoned/superseded records) lingering until the next compaction — and reads must consider multiple runs until merged.
- **Galaxy application:** this is the foundations §8 mark-compact pattern as a *continuous strategy*, and it is the right model for the fleet's append-mostly stores (jsonl ledgers, embed indexes, the tribal index). Deletions/supersessions should be tombstone-marked, not torn out in place; a background compaction merges runs, drops tombstoned + superseded records, and atomically swaps the offset index — copy-live-forward-then-free (applied-practice §2.4), never free-then-hope. This is also the structurally-correct answer to the tribal-index 512 MiB string-cap hazard (`182788232a`): compaction into sharded immutable runs avoids the single monolithic-rewrite wall.

### Technique 4.2 — Choose leveled vs tiered compaction by which amplification you can least afford
The compaction *policy* is a deliberate trade among write-, read-, and space-amplification: "In levelling, only one component exists per level, and merging happens more frequently, reducing the total number of components but increasing write amplification. In tiering, multiple components can coexist within a level, and merging occurs less frequently, reducing write amplification but increasing read costs because more components need to be searched" [Log-structured merge-tree, Wikipedia — confirmed].
- **WHEN an expert uses it:** at the design crossroads for any compacting store — pick the policy that protects your scarcest resource.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** **leveled** = more frequent merges → lower read/space amplification but HIGHER write amplification (good when reads dominate / disk is scarce). **Tiered** = less frequent merges → lower write amplification but HIGHER read amplification (good when writes/ingest dominate). You cannot minimize all three at once; choose which to sacrifice.
- **Galaxy application:** a write/ingest-heavy dormant-data store (a high-churn outcome ledger) should lean *tiered* (compact rarely, accept more runs to search) to avoid write-amplifying the fleet's NVMe; a read-heavy index queried every prompt (the tribal-rerank index) should lean *leveled* (compact more, keep few runs) to keep reads cheap. The choice is explicit and per-store — never a single global compaction setting.

## 5. Drive the whole lifecycle by policy, not just by access — autovacuum + ILM

### Technique 5.1 — In-place concurrent reclaim by default; full compaction only as a rare exception
PostgreSQL codifies the expert default: reclaim space for reuse *in place, concurrently*, and reserve whole-corpus rewriting for edge cases. Standard VACUUM "removes dead row versions... and marks the space available for future reuse" and "can run in parallel with production database operations," whereas VACUUM FULL "requires an ACCESS EXCLUSIVE lock... cannot be done in parallel," "actively compacts tables by writing a complete new version of the table file," and "administrators should strive to use standard VACUUM and avoid VACUUM FULL" [PostgreSQL routine vacuuming — confirmed].
- **WHEN an expert uses it:** routinely — in-place concurrent reclaim is the everyday tool; full compaction is the once-in-a-while defragmentation.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** in-place reclaim keeps the workload running and is cheap but leaves the store the same size (space marked reusable, not returned to the OS). Full compaction returns space + defragments but needs an exclusive lock and a full rewrite. Prefer concurrency; pay the exclusive-lock cost only when fragmentation actually demands it.
- **Galaxy application:** the dormant-data reclaimer's default is mark-space-reusable-in-place under concurrency (don't block the 26 slots); a full compacting rewrite of an index/corpus (the §4 merge that actually shrinks the file) is scheduled rarely, off-peak, and atomically — matching the applied-practice "throttle the reclaimer / don't stop-the-world" discipline.

### Technique 5.2 — Trigger reclamation from dead-data thresholds in the background, not on a fixed timer
Autovacuum is a policy-driven background reclaimer fired by accumulated-dead-tuple thresholds, distributed over time by a launcher/worker daemon: a table is vacuumed when "the number of tuples obsoleted since the last VACUUM exceeds the 'vacuum threshold'... defined as: vacuum threshold = ... vacuum base threshold + vacuum scale factor × number of tuples," and the launcher "will distribute the work across time" [PostgreSQL routine vacuuming — confirmed]. (The numeric base/scale-factor values are tuning constants — Owner-gated.)
- **WHEN an expert uses it:** when reclamation should be self-pacing — more dead data → more reclamation, proportional to table size, without an operator scheduling each run.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** a threshold-driven background reclaimer responds to actual garbage accumulation (vs a fixed timer that either over-runs on quiet stores or under-runs on busy ones) — at the cost of needing a tuned threshold and a launcher that fairly distributes work so no single sweep starves the workload.
- **Galaxy application:** the dormant-data sweep should fire on a *dead-fraction threshold* (e.g. "orphan/cold bytes exceed a scale-factor of the corpus") and self-distribute across the fleet's quiet windows — not on a blind cron interval. This is the strategy form of the applied-practice "reclamation has a deadline" lesson: proportional, background, self-pacing — never the unbounded full-throttle walk.

### Technique 5.3 — Information Lifecycle Management: tier + dispose by BUSINESS VALUE, not access frequency alone
The top-of-field lifecycle strategy moves beyond access-frequency HSM (foundations §2) to value/policy-driven management: ILM is "the policies, processes, practices, and tools used to align the business value of information with the most appropriate and cost-effective IT infrastructure from the time information is conceived through its final disposition" and is "dictated by business goals and drivers" rather than purely technical criteria [Information lifecycle management, Wikipedia — confirmed].
- **WHEN an expert uses it:** when data of equal access-temperature has UNEQUAL business value (a cold compliance record vs a cold disposable cache) and a one-axis temperature rule would mishandle one of them.
- **Trade-off DIRECTION:** value/policy-driven lifecycle is richer and safer (it never reaps a required-but-cold record, never over-retains a worthless-but-recently-touched one) at the cost of needing an explicit per-class value/retention policy on top of the access signal. More governance is traded for not losing required records.
- **Galaxy application:** this is the strategic capstone over foundations §10 (retention) + applied-practice §4.2 (retention-gap). The dormant-data ledger must classify on TWO axes — access-temperature (HSM/working-set) AND business-value/retention-class (ILM) — so JM Die customer/quote/financial/PII artifacts are tiered and disposed by mandated value/retention, never by access age alone. Temperature decides *dormant*; ILM policy decides *disposable*. (The actual retention windows and value classes are Owner-gated.)
