# Deep-Domain Research — PRISM Academy Galaxy (manufacturing pedagogy + curriculum)

Scope: competency frameworks (NIMS), DOL registered-apprenticeship standards, machining skill progression, instructional-design theory, and free CNC training curricula. All claims carry inline citations to free/legal public sources. This packet is research scaffolding for the `academy` galaxy's curriculum-design and skill-progression modeling — it informs how PRISM Academy should structure courses, sequence competencies, and assess mastery.

## NIMS — National Institute for Metalworking Skills credential framework

- **NIMS is a non-profit founded in 1995** to develop an industry standard for the machining and manufacturing trades; its credentials are used in recruitment by an estimated 6,000+ manufacturing companies. (NIMS / Lincoln Tech overview — https://www.nims-skills.org/ , https://www.lincolntech.edu/news/skilled-trades/cnc-machining-and-manufacturing/what-are-nims-certifications)
- **NIMS organizes credentials into three machining skill Levels (I, II, III)** across four major occupational groups (machining, tooling, metalforming, industrial maintenance); the levels are designed to define career paths and learning needs. (NIMS Machining Level I Standards PDF — https://www.nims-skills.org/sites/default/files/media/document/NIMS%20Machining%20Level%20I%20Standards.pdf)
- **There are eleven distinct Machining Level 1 certifications**, of which "Measurement, Materials, and Safety" is the most basic / foundational. (NIMS Machining Credentialing Starter Guide — https://www.nims-skills.org/sites/default/files/media/document/Starter%20Guide%20-%20Machining%20Credentialing%20v2.1_0.pdf)
- **NIMS uses a duty-based competency framework**: "duties" are the most important worker responsibilities; each duty is defined as a performance standard with accuracy requirements that must be achieved within specified times, plus the knowledge/academic skills needed to satisfy it. (NIMS Machining Level I Standards PDF — https://www.nims-skills.org/sites/default/files/media/document/NIMS%20Machining%20Level%20I%20Standards.pdf)
- **Most NIMS credentials require two components — a theory (knowledge) exam and a hands-on performance evaluation** — except "Measurement, Materials, and Safety," which requires only the theory exam. The performance evaluation has three forms: CAR (Credentialing Achievement Record / skills checks), Project (machine a NIMS part to print), or Performance Measure (observed workplace tasks). (NIMS Credentialing page — https://www.nims-skills.org/credentialing)
- **Exam scale examples (UNVERIFIED specifics — confirm against current NIMS exams):** Milling I is reported as a 56-question theory exam plus a committee-evaluated performance assessment; Grinding I as a 47-question exam plus panel-reviewed performance. (Indeed machinist-certification overview — https://www.indeed.com/career-advice/career-development/machinist-certification) NOTE: lima should re-verify question counts directly with NIMS — these come from a secondary career-advice source, not the NIMS standards PDF.

## DOL Registered Apprenticeship — the three measurement models (29 CFR Part 29)

- **Federal regulation (29 CFR §29.5) permits three ways to measure an apprenticeship term: time-based, competency-based, or hybrid.** Time-based requires completion of at least 2,000 hours of on-the-job learning (OJL) per the work-process schedule; competency-based measures verified skill demonstration with no fixed hours but a minimum 12-month duration; hybrid blends a minimum hour count with demonstrated competency. (eCFR 29 CFR Part 29 — https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-A/part-29 ; Cornell LII 29 CFR §29.5 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/29.5)
- **The choice of model rests with the program sponsor**, subject to approval by the Registration Agency as appropriate for the occupation. (eCFR 29 CFR Part 29 — https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-A/part-29)
- **DOL recommends a minimum of 144 hours of Related Technical Instruction (RTI) per year of apprenticeship** alongside the on-the-job learning. (eCFR 29 CFR Part 29 — https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-A/part-29 ; Urban Institute apprenticeship-standards explainer — https://www.urban.org/apps/youth-apprenticeship/registering-apprenticeship-standards)
- **"Competency" is regulatorily defined** as the attainment of manual, mechanical, or technical skills and knowledge specified by an occupational standard, demonstrated by written + hands-on proficiency measurement. (Cornell LII 29 CFR §29.5 — https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/29.5)
- **Competency-based / hybrid programs may issue interim credentials** that must clearly link to components of the apprenticeable occupation and define the assessment process for each. (eCFR 29 CFR Part 29 — https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-A/part-29)

## Machinist occupation standard (O*NET/SOC 51-4041.00, RAPIDS 0296)

- **The machinist trade is O*NET/SOC code 51-4041.00 with RAPIDS code 0296.** The full time-based machinist apprenticeship is commonly 8,000 hours of OJT. Sample state programs (e.g., Oregon, Alabama) list 8,000 OJT hours + 580 hours of Related Instruction. (Apprenticeship.gov occupation listing 51-4041.00 — https://www.apprenticeship.gov/apprenticeship-occupations/listings?occupationCode=51-4041.00 ; O*NET 51-4041.00 — https://www.onetonline.org/link/details/51-4041.00 ; apprenticeshipstandards.org 51-4041 results — https://apprenticeshipstandards.org/occupation_standards?q=51-4041)
- **Machinist work-process core competencies** (from a North Carolina competency-based program) include: machine safety + PPE usage; lock-out/tag-out + OSHA compliance; machine operations & material handling; hazardous-materials handling; part inspection; process control; single-part-production process adjustment; and manual layout operations. (apprenticeshipstandards.org Machinist CB occupation 0296CB — https://www.apprenticeshipstandards.org/occupation_standards/d9f1c146-07fa-4370-be4d-feef46851aa2)
- **A published competency-based machinist standard (0296CB) lets apprentices progress at their own pace** — they demonstrate competency via assessment rather than completing a fixed hour count. (apprenticeshipstandards.org Machinist 0296CB — https://www.apprenticeshipstandards.org/occupation_standards/d9f1c146-07fa-4370-be4d-feef46851aa2 ; USMAP/RAPIDS 0296D-CB — https://usmap.osd.mil/trades/competency/0296D-CB.pdf)

## Instructional-design theory — sequencing and assessing skill

- **Bloom's Taxonomy (1956, revised 2001) splits learning objectives into three domains: cognitive (knowledge), affective (emotion/attitude), and psychomotor (physical/action).** The cognitive levels were revised to Remember → Understand → Apply → Analyze → Evaluate → Create. (Wikipedia, Bloom's taxonomy — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloom's_taxonomy ; University of Waterloo CTE — https://uwaterloo.ca/centre-for-teaching-excellence/catalogs/tip-sheets/blooms-taxonomy)
- **The psychomotor domain is the most relevant to machining/hands-on training.** Bloom's original team left it underdeveloped; Elizabeth Simpson (1972) extended it into seven levels of psychomotor learning from simple perception up to origination of new movements. (Wikipedia, Bloom's taxonomy — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloom's_taxonomy ; NIU CITL — https://www.niu.edu/citl/resources/guides/instructional-guide/blooms-taxonomy.shtml)
- **Bloom levels are hierarchical — each level subsumes those before it** — which makes the taxonomy a tool for designing progressive, competency-based learning objectives that are observable and measurable. (University of Illinois Chicago CATE — https://teaching.uic.edu/cate-teaching-guides/syllabus-course-design/blooms-taxonomy-of-educational-objectives/)
- **The Dreyfus model of skill acquisition (Stuart & Hubert Dreyfus, 1980) defines five stages: Novice → Advanced Beginner → Competent → Proficient → Expert** (a sixth "Mastery" stage was added later). Learners shift from context-free rule-following (novice) toward holistic, intuitive performance (expert). (Wikipedia, Dreyfus model — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dreyfus_model_of_skill_acquisition ; Dreyfus 1980 five-stage report — https://devmts.org.uk/dreyfus.pdf)
- **Instructional implication of the Dreyfus model:** novices need clear step-by-step rules and foundational principles; competent learners need problem-solving + independent decision-making practice; experts should be leveraged for complex problems and mentoring. Instruction should be tailored per stage. (Umbrex tools-for-thinking summary — https://umbrex.com/resources/tools-for-thinking/what-is-the-dreyfus-model-of-skill-acquisition/ ; Mindtools — https://www.mindtools.com/atdbxer/the-dreyfus-model-of-skill-acquisition/)

## Workplace learning + deliberate practice (how OJT actually builds skill)

- **The 70-20-10 model (McCall, Lombardo & Eichinger, Center for Creative Leadership, 1980s) holds that ~70% of workplace learning comes from on-the-job experience, ~20% from social interaction/coaching/mentoring, and ~10% from formal training.** The ratios are guidelines, not laws, and empirical support is debated. (Training Industry wiki — https://trainingindustry.com/wiki/content-development/the-702010-model-for-learning-and-development/ ; ResearchGate study on the model — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/316610452_THE_702010_MODEL_FOR_LEARNING_AND_DEVELOPMENT_AN_EFFECTIVE_MODEL_FOR_CAPABILITY_DEVELOPMENT)
- **Ericsson's deliberate-practice research is the corrective to "experience alone makes experts."** Observed performance correlates only weakly with years of experience; expertise requires *structured* deliberate practice — focused effort on specific weaknesses, immediate feedback, repetition with refinement, and stretching beyond comfort. (Ericsson 2008, Academic Emergency Medicine — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1553-2712.2008.00227.x ; UCSF deliberate-practice pearls — https://meded.ucsf.edu/sites/meded.ucsf.edu/files/inline-files/pearls-deliberate-practice.pdf)
- **The "10,000-hour rule" (popularized by Gladwell from Ericsson et al. 1993) is widely misread** — Ericsson himself stated there is nothing magical about 10,000 hours; the *quality* of deliberate practice matters more than raw hour count, and required hours vary by domain and individual. Practice is necessary but not sufficient for expertise. (PMC review, Ericsson Krampe Tesch-Römer 1993 revisited — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6731745/ ; PMC, deliberate-practice evidence review — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7461852/)
- **Design implication for academy:** the 70% experiential portion only produces real skill when it carries deliberate-practice hallmarks (focused, feedback-rich, progressively harder). Routine repetition without these tends to produce automaticity and plateaued performance, not mastery. (Macnamara & Maitra 2019, Purdue — https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/macnamara-maitra-2019-the-role-of-deliberate-practice-in-expert-performance-revisiting-ericsson-krampe-tesch-romer.pdf)

## Free / legal curriculum corpora for the academy galaxy

- **MIT 2.810 "Manufacturing Processes and Systems"** is a hands-on graduate/upper-undergrad course covering assembly, machining, injection molding, casting, and thermoforming with emphasis on process physics, randomness, quality, rate, cost, and flexibility; materials are hosted on the departmental site (textbook: Kalpakjian & Schmid, *Manufacturing Engineering and Technology*, 7th ed.). (MIT 2.810 course site — https://web.mit.edu/2.810/www/ ; lecture 1 PDF — http://web.mit.edu/2.810/www/files/lectures/lec1-intro-2019.pdf)
- **MIT 2.008x "Fundamentals of Manufacturing Processes" (free via MITx/edX)** is the strongest free, structured online analog — covers machining, injection molding, casting, 3D printing, cost estimation, quality/variation, and sustainability with physics-first explanations. (MIT Open Learning — https://openlearning.mit.edu/courses-programs/mitx-courses/fundamentals-manufacturing-processes)
- **MIT OCW related free courses:** 2.008 Design and Manufacturing II (process physics, equipment/control, DFM, systems), 2.830J Control of Manufacturing Processes (statistical modeling, DOE, yield optimization), and 2.852 Manufacturing Systems Analysis (throughput, in-process inventory, scheduling). (MIT OCW 2.830J — https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-830j-control-of-manufacturing-processes-sma-6303-spring-2008/ ; MIT OCW 2.852 — https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-852-manufacturing-systems-analysis-spring-2010/)
- **NIMS curricula are delivered free/low-cost through accredited community colleges** that align coursework to NIMS standards and bundle the credential — examples: Ivy Tech (IN), Kirkwood (IA, Kibbie-Grant-eligible), Truckee Meadows (NV), Delaware County CC (PA, stackable single-skill-set design). Many states fund NIMS-aligned training via WIOA grants. (Ivy Tech NIMS CNC Operator — https://www.ivytech.edu/classes/skills-training-classes/manufacturing-industrial-technology-mfit/mfitnim5-nims-cnc-operator/ ; Kirkwood CNC Machining Technology — https://www.kirkwood.edu/programs/degrees/manufacturing-trades-transportation/cnc-machining-technology ; Truckee Meadows CNC CoA — https://catalog.tmcc.edu/degrees-certificates/computer-numeric-controlled-machining-ca/)
