# Academy Applied Practice

The **practitioner-knowledge** layer for the **academy** galaxy: the hard-won gotchas, failure modes, and technique decisions a world-class instructional designer / training practitioner carries that pure theory does not teach. This is DISTINCT from [[academy-pedagogy-foundations]] (the what-to-teach theory spine: competency frameworks, Bloom/Dreyfus, ADDIE, andragogy, NIST SPC) and from [[academy-source-atlas]] (the where-to-pull-fresh-material link directory). Read those first; this file does NOT repeat them. It captures the *judgment* — the places where the obvious design move is the wrong one, and what the expert does instead.

Every note below is: **the gotcha -> WHY it bites -> the expert's avoidance**, with the source confirmed inline. The constructs cited (cognitive load, spacing, transfer, the testing effect, etc.) are established learning-science literature; the failure modes are the practitioner's lived consequence of ignoring them.

## Common failure modes (the traps that look like good teaching)

- **Cognitive overload from "helpful" presentation — split-attention and redundancy.** The intuitive move is to add more — more labels, more on-screen text repeating the narration, a diagram on one page and its explanation on the next. The gotcha: presentation *format* itself "either promoted or limited learning," and separating related information or presenting redundant content "forces learners to mentally integrate material, wasting cognitive resources on coordination rather than understanding." WHY: working memory is finite; the coordination tax (extraneous load) is subtracted from the budget available for the actual concept. The expert physically *integrates* text into the diagram it describes, cuts narration-duplicating on-screen text, and treats every added element as a load cost to justify, not a free clarification. [src: [Cognitive load — Split-attention & redundancy effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_load)]

- **The worked-example effect reverses on experts (expertise reversal).** For a novice, "learners who studied worked examples were the most efficient" — showing a fully solved problem beats discovery learning. The gotcha is assuming that scaffold helps everyone: the advantage "disappears or reverses when learners gain expertise and find step-by-step solutions tedious," because the same guidance becomes redundant load. WHY: the support a novice needs is noise to someone who has internalized the procedure. The expert *fades the scaffold* as competence rises — full worked examples for the novice, completion problems for the advanced beginner, bare problems for the competent learner — rather than shipping one fixed level of hand-holding to a mixed cohort. [src: [Cognitive load — Worked-example & expertise reversal effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_load)]

- **Transfer failure: the learner who passed the test cannot do it on a real part (inert knowledge).** "Transfer of learning occurs when people apply information, strategies, and skills they have learned to a new situation or context" — and it routinely fails. The gotcha: knowledge stored in memory is "flexible, interpretive... and its recall and transfer are largely context-dependent," so a skill demonstrated in the classroom context does not automatically surface in the shop context. *Far* transfer (a substantially different situation) is much harder than *near* transfer (high overlap with training). WHY: without "mindfully abstracting knowledge through examining experiences for similarities," learners cannot generalize across contexts. The expert deliberately *varies the practice context* (different machines, materials, fixturing), names the underlying principle explicitly, and trains on conditions resembling the real job — not on a single clean idealized case. [src: [Transfer of learning — near/far transfer, context dependency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfer_of_learning)]

- **The expert-instructor blind spot (curse of knowledge).** Putting your best machinist in front of the class feels obviously right. The gotcha: the curse of knowledge is "a cognitive bias that occurs when a person who has specialized knowledge assumes that others share in that knowledge," and a "knowledgeable professor might no longer remember the difficulties that a student faces when learning a new subject." WHY: the expert cannot "accurately reconstruct what a person... without the knowledge would think," so they skip the very steps a novice trips on. It is even noted as "potentially ineffective, if not harmful, to think about how students are... learning material by asking the perspective of the teacher as opposed to what has been verified by students." The expert designer treats subject-matter mastery as necessary but not sufficient, *verifies difficulty against actual learners* (pilot, observe where they stall), and forces the SME to surface the implicit steps they no longer notice. [src: [Curse of knowledge — application to teaching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse_of_knowledge)]

## Technique decisions (where the comfortable choice is the wrong one)

- **Spacing beats massed practice — and the trap is that cramming *feels* better.** "More information is encoded into long-term memory by spaced study sessions... than by massed presentation (cramming)" — the effect is overwhelming: spaced practice outperformed massed practice "in 259 out of 271 cases," and the gains are robust "particularly when the spacing gaps are on the order of days or weeks." The gotcha: massed practice (a single all-day block) produces fluent in-the-moment performance, so both learners and schedulers read it as effective — while it fails at the day/week timescale that actually matters on the job. The expert schedules *distributed* revisits over days/weeks instead of one marathon session, even though the marathon looks more productive on the day. [src: [Spacing effect — distributed vs massed practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacing_effect)]

- **Use testing as a *teaching* tool, not just an assessment — and expect resistance.** The testing effect: "long-term memory is increased when part of the learning period is devoted to retrieving information from memory," and retrieval practice beats "only reading or passively studying material." The gotcha is two-sided perception: learners systematically undervalue it ("not seeing retrieval practice as a useful learning tool"), and "many teaching professionals remain unaware that testing functions optimally not as an assessment tool, but as a teaching tool." WHY: re-reading feels familiar and easy; effortful recall feels hard, so both sides avoid it. The expert builds frequent low-stakes retrieval *into* instruction (quick recall checks, closed-book worked recreation) rather than reserving testing for the terminal grade. [src: [Testing effect — retrieval practice, learner/instructor underestimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testing_effect)]

- **Desirable difficulty: conditions that make learning feel easy often make it worse.** "A desirable difficulty is a learning task that requires a considerable but desirable amount of effort, thereby improving long-term performance" — and "while difficult tasks might slow down learning initially, the long-term benefits are greater than with easy tasks." The gotcha (the fluency illusion): re-reading "feels productive because material becomes more recognizable, yet this doesn't guarantee deep processing or durable retention." WHY: subjective ease during study is a poor proxy for durable learning; learners and designers optimize for the feeling, not the outcome. The expert deliberately introduces retrieval, spacing, interleaving, and varied practice — accepting slower, harder-feeling acquisition for better retention and transfer — with one constraint: "tasks must remain achievable" (too hard discourages engagement). [src: [Desirable difficulty — the learning paradox, why easy methods fail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desirable_difficulty)]

- **Feedback timing is not "sooner is always better."** Among the desirable-difficulty techniques: "Counterintuitively, postponing feedback (while guaranteeing it arrives) strengthens learning more than immediate responses." The gotcha: the instinct to correct an error the instant it happens can short-circuit the learner's own retrieval/self-correction effort. WHY: immediate feedback can let the learner offload monitoring onto the instructor; a brief delay (with feedback *guaranteed*) preserves the effortful processing that builds durable memory. The expert chooses feedback latency on purpose — immediate for safety-critical/procedural errors where a wrong rep is dangerous, slightly delayed for conceptual practice where self-correction is the goal — and never leaves feedback open-loop (the "guaranteed it arrives" clause is load-bearing). [src: [Desirable difficulty — delayed feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desirable_difficulty)]

## Verification & assessment (proving the training actually worked)

- **Reliability is not validity — a consistent test can be consistently wrong.** A measurement can be reliable (repeatable) yet invalid (not measuring what it claims): "a scale that is 5 pounds off is reliable but not valid," and "just because a measure is reliable, it is not necessarily valid." Note the direction: "A test cannot be valid unless it is reliable" — reliability is necessary but insufficient for validity. The gotcha: a clean, repeatable multiple-choice exam can have high reliability and still fail to measure on-the-job competence (it measures recall of facts, not the skill). WHY: practitioners conflate "the test gives consistent scores" with "the test measures the right thing." The expert checks both — that the assessment is consistent *and* that it actually targets the competency the course exists to build (especially when the real target is a hands-on skill that a paper test cannot reach). [src: [Validity (statistics) — validity vs reliability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Validity_(statistics))]

- **Align assessment to objectives to instruction — or you grade something you never taught.** "Assessments should reveal how well students have learned what we want them to learn while instruction ensures that they learn it. For this to occur, assessments, learning objectives, and instructional strategies need to be closely aligned so that they reinforce one another." The gotcha: objectives, lessons, and the final exam drift apart — the objective says "set up and prove out a part," the lessons drill terminology, and the test asks for definitions — so a passing score certifies the wrong thing. WHY: each artifact is often authored separately and never cross-checked end to end. The expert designs backward from the objective: write the performance objective first, then the assessment that would prove it, then the instruction that prepares for that assessment — and audits the three for one-to-one alignment before launch. [src: [CMU Eberly Center — Align assessments, objectives, instructional strategies](https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/assessment/basics/alignment.html)]

## How this binds into PRISM academy

These gotchas are the *design discipline* layer over the foundations spine. Concretely: the cognitive-load + worked-example + expertise-reversal notes govern how an academy lesson presents a procedure (fade the scaffold by Dreyfus stage — see foundations §2). The spacing + testing + desirable-difficulty notes govern the academy module *cadence* — distributed retrieval checkpoints, not one marathon plus one terminal exam (this sharpens foundations §3's deliberate-practice + §5's ADDIE formative/summative split). The transfer + curse-of-knowledge notes govern who teaches and on what — vary the practice context toward real JM Die parts and verify difficulty against actual operators, not the SME's intuition. The validity/reliability + alignment notes govern the academy's *gate*: a hands-on performance evaluation (which NIMS already requires per foundations §1) exists precisely because a reliable paper test is not a valid measure of machining skill.

## Owner-gate (NOT promoted)

The following are deliberately LEFT for lima to bind against JM Die's actual program — they are numeric/contextual and must not be hardcoded from generic literature:
- **Specific spacing intervals** (how many days between revisits for a given academy module) — the literature confirms days-to-weeks helps but the optimal gap is task- and learner-dependent; tune empirically per course.
- **Optimal feedback-delay length** and which lessons get immediate vs delayed feedback — must be set per safety profile (safety-critical machining errors likely warrant immediate correction regardless of the general finding).
- **The achievability ceiling for desirable difficulty** (how hard is "too hard" for JM Die's actual operator population) — bound to the real cohort, who are experience-rich adult learners (foundations §5 andragogy).
- **Cut scores / pass thresholds and the exact form of the hands-on performance evaluation** — must map onto NIMS performance standards and JM Die's tolerances, not a generic rubric.
- Any **effect-size or percentage figure** beyond the one directly quoted with its source above (e.g. the 259/271 spacing result is reported as-cited, not generalized to a PRISM prediction).
