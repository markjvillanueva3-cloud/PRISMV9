## 1. Systems-theoretic hazard analysis — STPA/STAMP (the paradigm shift past component reliability)

This is the single highest-leverage advance over the foundations-level FMEA/risk-matrix view. The foundations entry treats hazards as things that *fail*; STAMP treats them as things that are *inadequately controlled* — a fundamentally more powerful frame for software-intensive, interaction-rich systems like PRISM.

### T1 — STAMP: model accidents as inadequate control, not (only) component failure
**Technique.** Nancy Leveson's STAMP (Systems-Theoretic Accident Model and Processes), from *Engineering a Safer World* (2012), reframes safety as a **control problem** rather than a component-reliability problem: accidents emerge from "inadequate control" and "unsafe interactions among non-failed components," where properly functioning parts interact dangerously ([Nancy Leveson, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy_Leveson)). Safety becomes *enforcing safety constraints* across the system's control structure, not driving each part's failure rate down.
**WHEN an expert uses it.** When the system is software-intensive and the dangerous scenarios are *interactions* (a controller acting on a stale model of the world) rather than a single part breaking — exactly the regime where FMEA's component-by-component catalog goes blind. Grounded historically in the TCAS and Therac-25 analyses, where no single component "failed" yet people died.
**Trade-off direction.** Far stronger coverage of emergent/interaction hazards and missing-feedback hazards; in exchange it costs more modeling effort up front and yields qualitative scenarios rather than a single ranked number — you trade a tidy RPN for a truer hazard set.
**Galaxy applies it:** PRISM's `S(x)` gate, the alarm surface, and the dispatchers form a control structure where every "non-failed" component can still combine into an unsafe job (a correct toolpath on the wrong units; a passing gate on a stale verdict). STAMP is the right lens for those — they are control-inadequacy hazards, invisible to a per-engine FMEA.

### T2 — STPA: the four-step systems-theoretic process analysis
**Technique.** STPA (System-Theoretic Process Analysis), the analysis method built on STAMP (Leveson & Thomas, *STPA Handbook*, 2018), runs four iterative steps: **(1) define the purpose** — name the *losses* (things of value to stakeholders), the *hazards* (system states that with worst-case conditions lead to a loss), and the *system-level constraints*; **(2) model the hierarchical control structure** as feedback control loops of controllers, controlled processes, actuators, sensors, each controller carrying a *process model*; **(3) identify Unsafe Control Actions (UCAs)** — the context in which a control action causes a hazard; **(4) identify loss scenarios** — the causal factors tracing how each UCA occurs, including scenarios where the action is issued unsafely *and* where it is issued correctly but improperly executed ([STPA Handbook, summarized — York Assuring-Autonomy courseware](https://www.york.ac.uk/media/assuring-autonomy/bodyofknowledgestructure/section1imagesanddocs/1.2%20cross%20domain%20practical%20guidance%20SUCCESS.pdf); [systems-theoretic process analysis applied — peer-reviewed, arXiv:1912.02019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.02019)).
**WHEN an expert uses it.** Early and iteratively across the lifecycle of a complex/autonomous system, and increasingly for AI-driven systems where the hazardous behavior is "the controller did the wrong thing for a plausible reason," not "the chip broke."
**Trade-off direction.** Step 2 (the control-structure model) is the expensive part and the source of most of the value — skimping it collapses STPA back toward a checklist. More modeling, more found scenarios, fewer surprises in operation.
**Galaxy applies it:** model the compliance-safety galaxy as a control structure (operator → dispatcher → engine → gate → job) and the "missing-feedback" / "stale process model" loss scenarios fall out — e.g. a gate whose process model says "units known" when they are not. That is a Step-4 loss scenario, and it is the unit's *design requirement*, not an afterthought.

### T3 — Unsafe Control Actions: the four ways a control action is unsafe
**Technique.** In STPA Step 3, a control action becomes unsafe in exactly four ways: **(1) not provided** when needed → hazard; **(2) provided** when it causes a hazard; **(3) provided too early / too late / out of order**; **(4) applied too long or stopped too soon** ([STPA Handbook four-UCA categories, summarized — arXiv:1912.02019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.02019); cross-confirmed [arXiv:2506.01782 STPA for frontier AI](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.01782)). Thomas's *context tables* (process-model-variable combinations) formalize the "in which context" so the analysis is systematic, not ad hoc.
**WHEN an expert uses it.** Whenever you have a defined control action and need to enumerate its unsafe contexts exhaustively — this is the generative engine of STPA and the part that catches *timing and duration* hazards FMEA and the risk matrix structurally cannot express.
**Trade-off direction.** The four-category sweep is cheap to apply once the control structure exists and is the highest-yield step — it surfaces "right action, wrong time" hazards that component-failure methods never name.
**Galaxy applies it:** apply the four UCA guide-words to every safety-relevant action the galaxy issues — gate-pass *not provided* (hazard: a real risk un-flagged), gate-pass *provided in an unsafe context* (the fail-open class from applied-practice G2), an alarm *too late* to act on, an isolation *stopped too soon*. The four categories are a ready-made coverage checklist for the gate's own design review.

## 2. Complementary classical hazard analyses — pick the method to the question

An expert does not run one method; they pick the analysis whose *direction* matches the question, and run them as independent cross-checks (this is defense-in-depth applied to the *analysis itself*).

### T4 — Fault Tree Analysis: top-down deductive, with minimal cut sets
**Technique.** FTA starts from an undesired **top event** and works *backward deductively* to the combinations of contributing causes ([Fault tree analysis, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_tree_analysis)). It is the deductive complement to FMEA's bottom-up inductive sweep (foundations §4): FTA "excels at systemic failure patterns but struggles identifying all possible initiating faults"; FMEA "comprehensively catalogs component failures but lacks system-level analysis." Its key product is the **minimal cut set** — the smallest combination of events that causes the top event, where removing any one breaks the failure path. Single-event cut sets *are* your single points of failure.
**WHEN an expert uses it.** When you already know the catastrophic outcome you must prevent and need to prove the system is resilient to single *and multiple* failures, and to find the smallest failure combinations to guard. Standard in aerospace, nuclear, process/chemical.
**Trade-off direction.** Strong on multi-failure/system resilience and on exposing SPOFs as one-element cut sets; weak on *discovering* the initiating faults in the first place — pair it with an inductive method (FMEA) or a systems-theoretic one (STPA) so the top events you analyze are the right ones.
**Galaxy applies it:** for a named catastrophic compliance-safety outcome ("an un-verified program ships"), build the fault tree — the one-element cut sets are precisely the SPOF gates applied-practice G4 warns about, now found systematically instead of by intuition.

### T5 — HAZOP: guide-word + parameter deviation, team-based
**Technique.** HAZOP (Hazard and Operability study) systematically combines **guide words** (No, More, Less, As-well-as, Part-of, Reverse, Other-than) with **process parameters** (flow, temperature, pressure, level, composition, time...) to generate credible **deviations** at each system **node**, then reviews each deviation's causes, consequences, and safeguards — run by a multidisciplinary team with an independent facilitator ([Hazard and operability study, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazard_and_operability_study)).
**WHEN an expert uses it.** Relatively early through detailed design (and as a revalidation tool in operation) for parametrized/process-like systems — regulators treat it as a near-mandatory detailed-design step in process industries.
**Trade-off direction.** The guide-word grid forces *completeness* over a node's parameter space (its strength) at the cost of being labor-intensive and team-heavy; it is weaker for systems whose hazards are software-logic interactions rather than parameter deviations (reach for STPA there).
**Galaxy applies it:** the guide-word discipline generalizes to a pipeline's data nodes — "No units," "Reverse feed direction," "Other-than material," "More engagement than specified" are deviations the compliance-safety galaxy should enumerate per operation. It is a structured way to make the hazard-ID of foundations §1 *exhaustive over a node* rather than ad hoc.

### T6 — Bow-tie analysis: barriers between a central event, threats, and consequences
**Technique.** A bow-tie diagram puts the **central hazardous event** (the "knot," where control of an energy source is lost) in the middle, **threats** with their **preventive barriers** on the left, and **consequences** with their **mitigative/recovery barriers** on the right — a qualitative fusion of fault-tree (cause side) and event-tree (consequence side) logic that makes the *barriers/controls* the visible objects ([Bow-tie diagram, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bow-tie_diagram)).
**WHEN an expert uses it.** For rapid risk scoping, communication/training, and identifying *control gaps* — it shows at a glance whether a threat path has any preventive barrier and whether a consequence path has any recovery barrier.
**Trade-off direction.** Trades the quantitative rigor of full fault/event trees for accessibility and barrier-visibility; use it to *find missing barriers fast*, then deepen the critical paths with FTA/STPA — do not use the bow-tie's simplicity as the final quantitative analysis.
**Galaxy applies it:** map the central event "unsafe job reaches the machine," with PRISM's independent layers as the left/right barriers (hazard-ID, `S(x)`, alarm, hierarchy-of-controls recommendation, audit trail). A threat path with no preventive barrier, or a consequence path with no recovery barrier, is the bow-tie's name for a defense-in-depth gap — directly actionable.

## 3. Defeating common-mode failure — independence is the whole game

The applied-practice sibling warns that non-independent layers are "one layer wearing a costume" (G5). The *advanced* move is to attack correlated failure with a dedicated method, and to architect independence rather than assume it.

### T7 — Common-cause failure (CCF) analysis: hunt the shared root that defeats redundancy
**Technique.** A common-cause / common-mode failure is when "failures in multiple parts of a system may be caused by a single fault" — the classic example being all redundant pumps in one room failing together from a single heat source ([Common cause and special cause, Wikipedia — engineering section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_cause_and_special_cause_(statistics))). CCF introduces dependency between events that *invalidates* the independent-failure math redundancy relies on. The advanced technique is to actively enumerate shared causes — shared power, shared environment, shared component batch, shared software library, shared input parser, shared design assumption, shared author — and architect them out or down.
**WHEN an expert uses it.** Any time a design claims redundancy or "N independent layers" — CCF analysis is what *earns* the word "independent" instead of asserting it.
**Trade-off direction.** Designing out common causes (diverse implementations, separated environments, different data sources) costs more than cloning a layer, but cloning a layer buys *zero* additional protection against the shared root — the cost buys real coverage, the clone buys a costume.
**Galaxy applies it:** before counting two compliance-safety checks as two layers, run the CCF question — do they read the same field via the same parser, share the same env assumption, share the same author's blind spot? If yes, they are one layer (this is the `runout double-count` shape from CLAUDE.md inverted: co-varying "redundant" checks cancel to one). Diversity must be deliberate.

### T8 — Architect the safety function independent of the thing it protects
**Technique.** In IEC 61508/61511 practice, the Safety Instrumented System (SIS) implementing a Safety Instrumented Function (SIF) "must be independent from all other control systems that control the same equipment, in order to ensure SIS functionality is not compromised" — the protection layer is deliberately separated from the Basic Process Control System (BPCS) it backstops ([Safety instrumented system, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safety_instrumented_system)). The safety layer does not share the failure modes of the system whose failures it exists to catch.
**WHEN an expert uses it.** Whenever a protective function backstops a primary control function — the backstop must not fail for the same reason, at the same time, as the thing it protects.
**Trade-off direction.** A separate, independent safety channel costs more (extra hardware/logic/maintenance) than folding the check into the primary controller, but a check living *inside* the system it guards goes down *with* that system — buying independence buys protection precisely at the moment it is needed.
**Galaxy applies it:** the compliance-safety `S(x)` gate should not be implemented inside the same engine/parser/process whose output it validates — a verdict computed by the producer it is checking is a BPCS-checks-itself anti-pattern. Keep the gate's evidence path and the produced artifact's path independent so a common bug cannot pass and self-approve in one stroke.

## 4. The safe state must be named, reachable, and provable — beyond "fail-safe"

Applied-practice G1 establishes that "fail-safe" is meaningless until the safe state is *named*. The advanced layer adds two requirements the gotcha does not: the safe state must be **reachable** under the failure that triggers it, and the design's safety must be **argued, not assumed**.

### T9 — Fail-CLOSED with a named, reachable safe state as the default branch
**Technique.** Combining the foundations defense-in-depth requirement with the applied-practice fail-open lesson into a *design rule*: a safety-relevant function's default branch and every exception handler resolve to the *named* safe state (for a manufacturing gate: DENY / STOP), and that safe state must be *reachable* by the system in the failure mode that invokes it — a "safe state" the system cannot actually enter when its trigger fires is not a safe state. This is the design-rule synthesis of [fail-safe / fail-closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fail-safe) (which the sibling cites for the door analogy) and the defense-in-depth "if one layer fails the next still protects" requirement.
**WHEN an expert uses it.** At every safety-relevant branch — the *affirmative, evidenced success path* is the only route to "allow"; everything else (error, timeout, missing evidence, novel input) routes to the named safe state.
**Trade-off direction.** Defaulting closed sacrifices availability for safety (the system stops when the safety component is unsure) — the *correct* sacrifice for a gate, and the explicit, audited inverse of the convenient fail-open default applied-practice G3 warns about.
**Galaxy applies it:** every `catch`, timeout, and unknown-input branch in the compliance-safety path returns DENY/abstain and fails loud — never `return pass` / `return empty`. And the "safe state" (refuse the job, hold the machine) must be one the surrounding system can actually honor when the gate fires; an unenforceable DENY is a fail-open in disguise.

### T10 — The safety case: argue safety with claims-arguments-evidence (GSN)
**Technique.** A safety case is "a structured argument, supported by evidence, intended to justify that a system is acceptably safe" for a given application and environment, typically expressed in **Goal Structuring Notation (GSN)** as a claims-arguments-evidence tree, and increasingly integrated with model-based engineering and formal methods for software-intensive systems ([Safety case, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safety_case)). It is the *goal-based* alternative to purely prescriptive compliance — you do not assume "followed the process ⇒ safe," you *argue* why the system is safe and attach the evidence.
**WHEN an expert uses it.** In regulated/high-consequence domains (aviation, automotive, rail, medical devices, defense) and whenever prescriptive checklists cannot capture a novel/complex architecture — the safety case makes the reasoning explicit and reviewable, and the UK framing ties acceptance to keeping risk ALARP (foundations §3).
**Trade-off direction.** Building and maintaining a living argument costs more than ticking a checklist, but it surfaces *unstated assumptions* and *evidence gaps* a checklist hides — and a checkbox-passed system with no argument for *why* it is safe is exactly the prescriptive trap a safety case exists to escape.
**Galaxy applies it:** the compliance-safety galaxy *is* PRISM's safety-case spine — the audit trail (foundations §6) supplies the *evidence*, and the gate decisions supply the *claims*. The advanced move is to make the connecting *argument* explicit (claim: "this job is acceptably safe" ← arguments: hazard-ID complete, controls at the right hierarchy level, gate passed on affirmative evidence ← evidence: the audit records). A gate verdict with no traceable argument is a claim with no support.

## 5. Hazards without a fault — the frontier (SOTIF / functional insufficiency)

The newest advance, and the one most relevant to PRISM's AI/ML-driven surfaces: a system can be hazardous *while working exactly as designed*. Classical functional safety (IEC 61508 / ISO 26262, foundations §3) only addresses *malfunctions*; this leaves a whole class of risk unaddressed.

### T11 — SOTIF (ISO 21448): treat functional insufficiency as a first-class hazard source
**Technique.** SOTIF (Safety Of The Intended Functionality, ISO 21448) addresses "the absence of unreasonable risk due to hazards resulting from functional insufficiencies of the intended functionality or reasonably foreseeable misuse" — i.e. hazards that arise *even when no component has failed and the system behaves as designed*, because the design/specification itself is insufficient or the implementation cannot meet its spec under all conditions ([ISO 21448:2022, ISO.org](https://www.iso.org/standard/77490.html); corroborated by multiple industry references via [SOTIF vs ISO 26262 overview](https://www.cscanada.ca/sotif-introduction/)). A latent **functional insufficiency** becomes a hazard when activated by a **triggering condition** (lighting, edge-case input, foreseeable misuse). Sensor limits, algorithm/edge-case limits, and incomplete real-world coverage are the canonical sources.
**WHEN an expert uses it.** For ML/perception/AI-driven and complex-sensing systems where situational awareness comes from algorithms that can produce a wrong-but-not-failed output — SOTIF *complements* (does not replace) functional safety; a system can be fully ISO-26262/61508 compliant and still carry unaddressed SOTIF risk.
**Trade-off direction.** SOTIF demands the harder, never-"done" work of finding *triggering conditions* and edge-case insufficiencies (the unknown-unsafe space) rather than the cleaner work of bounding component failure rates — but skipping it leaves the entire "works as designed yet hazardous" class invisible to a classical safety analysis.
**Galaxy applies it:** PRISM's AI-driven safety/advisory surfaces (the GNN tier-5 classifier, OCR/vision extraction, LLM-routed reasoning) can emit a *confident wrong* output with no fault anywhere — a SOTIF hazard, not a functional-safety one. The compliance-safety galaxy should treat model functional-insufficiency (low-confidence-but-emitted, out-of-distribution input, foreseeable-misuse prompts) as a named hazard class with its own triggering-condition hunt, distinct from "did the engine throw." This is why the gate must abstain below its confidence band rather than emit (the selective-deploy discipline already in PRISM's GNN tier-5).
