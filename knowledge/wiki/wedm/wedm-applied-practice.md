## Common failure modes

### 1. Wire breakage — thermal stress, electrical overload, or wrong tension
**Gotcha (CONFIRMED, [Xometry — Wire EDM Machining](https://www.xometry.com/resources/machining/wire-edm-machining/)):** even on a healthy machine "wire breakage can still occur due to thermal stress, electrical overload, or improper tension." **Why:** the wire is the consumable electrode being eroded in the same gap it is cutting — push too much discharge energy or too much tension into a single segment and it parts. **Expert avoidance:** the wire is run as a continuously-fed consumable — "the wire is wound between two spools so that the active part of the wire is constantly changing" so no single length absorbs cumulative erosion ([Wikipedia — Electrical discharge machining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_discharge_machining)). Treat a break as a symptom (over-energy, flushing starvation, or tension error), not a random event — diagnose the gap state before re-threading and re-running.

### 2. Flushing starvation → short circuit and abnormal discharge
**Gotcha (CONFIRMED, peer-reviewed [PMC6384716 — Ultrasonic Vibration Assisted EDM, An Overview](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6384716/)):** when eroded debris accumulates in the gap it "decreases the resistance in this gap," which "becomes the reason behind the occurrences of abnormal discharges in the short circuit" — and the downstream effect is a "significant increase in tool wear" with slower material removal. **Why:** debris bridges the gap, so the controller no longer sees clean isolated sparks but a degrading conductive path. **Expert avoidance:** "quick cleansing of debris from the sparking gap... plays a crucial role on the process performance" — keep flushing effective (good dielectric circulation), and read rising short-circuit / retract behaviour as a *flushing* problem distinct from an under-energy condition.

### 3. Poor flushing → arcing → lost dimensional accuracy and surface integrity
**Gotcha (CONFIRMED, peer-reviewed [PMC6909068 — A systematic review on powder-mixed EDM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6909068/)):** "the presence of debris in the inter-electrode gap reduces process efficiency and might initiate arcing which deteriorates dimensional accuracy" and surface integrity. **Why:** arcing concentrates energy on debris instead of cleanly eroding the programmed path — the part comes out the wrong size with a worse finish, not just slower. **Expert avoidance:** flushing *mode* is a first-class setup decision — the literature names six methods ("Side flushing, Suction through the electrode, Pressure through the electrode, Jet flushing, Vacuum flushing, Injection flushing"), and modern machines add "servo-controlled cyclic reciprocation or vibrating tool electrode which will create a hydraulic pumping action that helps chip removal." Match the flushing approach to the part geometry; the same discharge settings behave differently under good vs. starved flushing.

### 4. Recast layer + residual tensile stress on the cut face
**Gotcha (CONFIRMED, [Wikipedia — EDM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_discharge_machining)):** "A recast layer is formed at the cut surface due to melting of the material by the arc," and the thermal cycle causes "formation of a recast layer on the part and residual tensile stresses." **Why:** the surface is melted and re-solidified, not sheared — it is metallurgically different from the bulk and can carry tensile stress and microcracks. **Expert avoidance:** the same source notes "little change in the mechanical properties" results only when "energy per pulse is relatively low (as in finishing operations)" — i.e. the rough cut owns the recast/HAZ, and finer finish (skim) passes at lower energy are what reduce it. This is the surface-integrity rationale behind the multi-pass strategy in §6.

## Setup & fixturing gotchas

### 5. Material moves when it is cut free — residual stress distortion
**Gotcha (CONFIRMED, [Wikipedia — EDM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_discharge_machining), residual-stress mechanism):** EDM imposes "a significant thermal cycle" leaving "residual tensile stresses." Combined with any *pre-existing* residual stress in the stock (from rolling/heat-treat), the inner slug or the part can shift the instant the wire severs it from the parent block — the dimension that was perfect mid-cut is wrong once it is free. **Why:** locked-in stress is released the moment the constraining material is removed. **Expert avoidance (decision-logic):** stress-relieve / heat-treat the blank *before* finish wire-EDM where the application demands it, and sequence the cut so the part stays supported as long as possible (leave tabs / cut the critical features last). Treat "the part will not move when cut free" as an assumption to verify per-material, not a given.

### 6. Threading the wire requires a start hole for any closed internal cut
**Gotcha (CONFIRMED, [Wikipedia — EDM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_discharge_machining)):** "On wire-cut EDM machines, small hole drilling EDM is used to make a through hole in a workpiece through which to thread the wire for the wire-cut EDM operation." **Why:** for an enclosed pocket/aperture the wire is a closed loop that must pass *through* the part — with no edge to start from, you need a pre-drilled start hole. **Expert avoidance:** plan the start hole into the part/program for every internal feature; for unattended (lights-out) running, reliable auto-threading at that hole — and clean flushing at the thread point — is what lets the machine re-thread and continue after a break without an operator present. A missing or poorly-placed start hole is a job that cannot run, not a job that runs slowly.

### 7. Workpiece must be electrically conductive — hardness is irrelevant, conductivity is the gate
**Gotcha (CONFIRMED, [Xometry](https://www.xometry.com/resources/machining/wire-edm-machining/) + [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_discharge_machining)):** "The material must be conductive for the process to work" / "Wire EDM is limited to metals that conduct electricity." **Why:** removal is by spark erosion, so the work has to complete the discharge circuit — a non-conductor cannot sustain the spark regardless of how machinable it looks geometrically. **Expert avoidance (decision-logic):** gate feasibility on conductivity *first*. Hardened tool steel and tungsten carbide are ideal EDM candidates precisely because the process is thermal, not mechanical — but a non-conductive ceramic is out-of-process (or needs a special assisting-electrode setup) no matter the geometry. Do not confuse "too hard to mill" (a good EDM fit) with "non-conductive" (a hard stop).

### 8. Dielectric water conductivity must be actively controlled
**Gotcha (CONFIRMED, [Wikipedia — EDM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_discharge_machining)):** "The wire-cut process uses water as its dielectric fluid, controlling its resistivity and other electrical properties with filters and PID controlled de-ionizer units." **Why:** the water must *insulate* the gap until breakdown — if it drifts too conductive (contaminated, deionizer resin spent), the gap stops behaving as a clean dielectric and cutting destabilizes. **Expert avoidance:** treat the deionizer/filtration loop as part of the cutting system, not plumbing — a maintenance lapse here shows up as unexplained instability and poor finish, not as an obvious fault. (The specific target resistivity is a number — owner-gated.)

## Technique decisions

### 9. Speed vs. accuracy vs. finish is a true three-way trade — you cannot have all
**Decision (CONFIRMED, [Modern Machine Shop — Buying a Wire EDM, Part 3](https://www.mmsonline.com/articles/buying-a-wire-edm-speed-accuracy-and-finish)):** "You don't get speed with precision, and you can't achieve high accuracy without also achieving a fine surface finish." **Why:** accuracy and surface finish are coupled (both improved by finer, lower-energy sparks), and both conflict with raw cutting speed. **Expert avoidance of the wrong choice:** decide up front whether the job is throughput-bound or precision-bound and set the pass plan accordingly — chasing all three at once is the error. (The trade-off *direction* is promoted; any quoted Ra/speed figures are owner-gated.)

### 10. Multi-pass: rough fast, then progressively lighter skim passes
**Decision (CONFIRMED, [Modern Machine Shop — Buying a Wire EDM, Part 3](https://www.mmsonline.com/articles/buying-a-wire-edm-speed-accuracy-and-finish)):** the first pass is a roughing pass that cuts as fast as possible at the expense of finish; then "each subsequent skim cut travels at progressively faster speeds, takes less and less material while steadily improving dimensional accuracy and quality." **Why:** the rough pass removes bulk (and owns the recast/HAZ); each skim removes a thinner layer at lower energy, tightening size and refining finish. **Expert technique:** during finish cuts "the tension on the wire is increased, the current is reduced, and the voltage gap narrowed, allowing the user to refine the spark and the distance the spark jumps." More skim passes buy a finer finish — but at diminishing return, so the pass *count* is chosen from the finish requirement, not maximized blindly. (The exact pass count for a given finish is a number — owner-gated.)

### 11. Material softness can defeat finish even when speed is easy
**Gotcha (CONFIRMED, [Modern Machine Shop — Buying a Wire EDM, Part 3](https://www.mmsonline.com/articles/buying-a-wire-edm-speed-accuracy-and-finish)):** "Although aluminum is easy to cut at higher speeds, the material is so soft that it is very difficult to get a good surface finish." **Why:** the easy-cutting material is not automatically the easy-finishing material — softness works against the final surface. **Expert avoidance:** set finish expectations per-material; do not assume a fast-cutting alloy will also hit a fine-finish target, and budget extra skim passes (or accept a coarser finish) accordingly.

### 12. Taper error from differential traverse speed — and why you must test-cut to compensate
**Gotcha (CONFIRMED, [MoldMaking Technology — Taper Angles and Wire EDM](https://www.moldmakingtechnology.com/articles/taper-angles-and-wire-edm)):** on a tapered cut "slower motion keeps the wire in any given place longer than faster traverse speeds would," so the slower-moving end over-erodes; the result is "the radius actually will be smaller at the top than what was programmed, and the angle from top to bottom will now not be to program size." **Why:** the upper (U/V) axes traverse a different distance than the lower (X/Y) axes for the same profile, so dwell time — and therefore erosion — differs top vs. bottom. **Expert avoidance (decision-logic):** taper compensation is **not** a constant. The expert "performs a first cut, measures and records the measurements, and then enters the compensation value" using the *actual* workpiece material and setup — measuring at the start, mid-point, and end of each segment on both profiles. Any wedm taper engine must take a calibration input from a test cut, never hardcode an angular-error figure.

## Verification

### 13. Thermal stability of the machine environment is a hard precision gate
**Gotcha (CONFIRMED, [Modern Machine Shop — Buying a Wire EDM, Part 3](https://www.mmsonline.com/articles/buying-a-wire-edm-speed-accuracy-and-finish)):** steel and carbide expand with temperature, so holding tight tolerance on larger parts requires holding the *ambient* shop temperature within a narrow window over the full cut. **Why:** a multi-hour wire-EDM cut on a long part will drift dimensionally if the room temperature swings — the machine and part grow/shrink under you. **Expert avoidance:** for precision work, treat ambient temperature control as part of the process capability, not a comfort setting; verify the environment is stable before trusting a tight measurement off a long, slow cut. (The expansion coefficient and the specific temperature window are numbers — owner-gated.)

### 14. Verify size on the *finished* (skimmed) part, and verify the recast was reduced
**Decision (synthesized from CONFIRMED §4 + §10):** because the rough pass owns the recast/residual-stress layer and the skim passes are what reduce it, final dimensional *and* surface-integrity verification must be against the post-skim surface — never the rough cut. **Why:** measuring or accepting off the rough pass reports neither the final size (skims still move it) nor the final surface condition (recast not yet reduced). **Expert avoidance:** confirm the finish pass actually ran to the planned count and inspect for recast/microcrack risk on critical faces, especially on hardened tool steel where the recast layer is most consequential.
